AI-Driven Gastroenterology: Transforming Endoscopy, Diagnosis, and Training
  • Source: ASG Annual Meeting Session 5100
  • Topic: Artificial Intelligence Applications in Gastroenterology Endoscopy, Diagnosis, and Medical Training
  • Author(s): Wallace M., Kushner V., Waxman I., Chandrasekar V., Stidham R., Kaswani R.
1. AI in Barrett's Esophagus: Detection and Risk Stratification
1.1. Background on Barrett's esophagus progression
  • Barrett's → Low Grade Dysplasia (LGD): High risk of progression to high grade dysplasia
  • High Grade Dysplasia → Adenocarcinoma: ~10% per year progression rate
  • Barrett's is treatable --> intervention can change natural history
1.2. Seattle Biopsy Protocol Limitations
  • Standard: 4-quadrant biopsies, 1cm above SCJ every 1cm to GE junction
  • Problem: Longer segments --> ↓ adherence to protocol (Abraham's study)
  • Clinicians frequently miss dysplasia in long segments
1.3. Inspection Time Impact
  • Spending 1 minute per centimeter of Barrett's: detection ↑ from 13% to 54%
  • Current practice often inadequate inspection time
1.4. Expert vs. General Gastroenterologist Staging Accuracy
  • Superficial (M1/M2) lesion detection: high accuracy maintained
  • Intramucosal vs. submucosal cancer differentiation:
  • Sensitivity only 43% for experienced international experts
  • Need for advanced imaging + AI to improve characterization
1.5. Technologies Evaluated (VASG Threshold)
  • Only 2 technologies passed clinical practice thresholds:
  • High-resolution endoscopy + acetic acid or NBI (digital chromoendoscopy)
  • Other technologies evaluated but not adopted widely
1.6. AI Detection Performance (Bergman Group - Gastroenterology 2020)
  • CAD-AI system: sensitivity/specificity >90% for dysplasia detection
  • General gastroenterologists without AI:
  • Median sensitivity: 74%
  • Specificity: >90%
  • With AI assistance, generalists perform like Barrett's experts
1.7. CAD-AI Impact on Individual Performance
  • Scattered plot analysis shows:
  • Unassisted physicians: inconsistent/uncertain detection patterns
  • AI-assisted: nearly all operators reach ~90% detection rate
  • Performance curves dramatically improved across all skill levels
1.8. Systems Evaluated
  • GI Genius (Dutch system): green boxes for dysplastic areas
  • German Mainframe System (Bego et al.): similar performance improvements
  • Both systems show significant improvement in dysplasia detection
1.9. First In-Vivo Pilot Study (DODGE System)
  • Detection on single pass vs. every 2cm assessment:
  • Single pass: 100% sensitivity, 53% specificity
  • Every 2cm: 100% sensitivity, 73% specificity
  • First real-time patient study (not just video/image analysis)
1.10. Heat Map Visualization
  • AI highlights flat lesions near SCJ that may be missed visually
  • Example: erythematous area below squamous island flagged precisely
  • Allows targeted focal therapy for concerning regions
1.11. Natural Language Processing (NLP) for Surveillance
  • Automated extraction from endoscopy + pathology reports
  • AI determines surveillance intervals without human review
  • Accuracy vs. guidelines: ~90%
  • Feeds directly into EMR for surveillance scheduling
1.12. Meta-Analysis (2020) - AI Across GI Conditions
  • Evaluated: gastric cancer, squamous dysplasia, Barrett's, H. pylori
  • All conditions showed AUC close to 1.0 (near-perfect detection)
  • Operating characteristic curves consistently excellent
1.13. Applications Beyond Barrett's
  • Gastric cancer: AI comparable to Japanese experts for superficial SCC detection
  • Eosinophilic Esophagitis (EoE): AI helps trainees/consultants identify EoE changes
  • Heat map shows exudates and rings for EoE classification
2. AI in Pancreaticobiliary Disorders
2.1. EUS Imaging Analysis Development
  • Inflection point: past 5 years with computing power advances
  • EUS FNA sensitivity for small lesions (<10mm): 82% with 91% accuracy
  • Opportunity for quality metrics (e.g., % of pancreas visualized)
2.2. EUS AI for Mass Characterization (Mayo - Neil/M Levy)
  • Unique dataset: 583 patients with both videos and images
  • Wide spectrum of pathology included
  • AI sometimes identified AIP based on bile duct wall thickening (concomitant cholangiopathy)
  • AI outperformed physicians in sensitivity while maintaining specificity
2.3. Japanese Study - Pancreatic Cancer Detection (772 patients)
  • Single-center retrospective model
  • Used data augmentation to correct 80% cancer imbalance
  • Achieved high sensitivity for detecting pancreatic cancer
  • Limitation: external validation needed
2.4. Multimodality Integration (China Study)
  • Combined: lab values, history, radiology, EUS examination
  • Ensemble model enhanced AI detection for cancer
  • Crossover trial design
2.5. Lesion Detection - Tandem Study (Published at Meeting)
  • 36 EUS videos with large number of endosonographers
  • Without AI vs. with AI comparison
  • Reviewer accuracy: 77% with AI detection (improvement shown)
2.6. Real-Time EUS-AI - Recent Publication
  • Model built for detecting and segmenting pancreatic mass lesions
  • Similar to expert annotations for identification
  • Published within past few months in Endoscopy
2.7. Pancreatic Cystic Lesions - ICG Guidelines Challenges
  • International consensus guidelines for IPMN differentiation
  • Performance characteristics suboptimal
  • Retrospective study: 200 patients, only 50 used, generated 3,970 still images
2.8. Cystic Lesion AI Performance
  • Sensitivity: similar to human operator
  • Specificity: 92.6% vs. human operator only 22%
  • Data augmentation used to balance classes
2.9. Confocal Needle Probe (nCLE) + AI for Cysts
  • Real-time cyst content interpretation is difficult
  • AI reduces time per IPMN assessment by 4 minutes
  • Classifies papillary fronds for IPMN vs. non-IPMN
2.10. Dysplasia Detection in Resected Cystic Lesions (63 patients)
  • nCLE-based AI identifies papillary projections
  • Thin papillary projections --> low grade dysplasia
  • Thick dark/white bands --> high grade dysplasia
  • Adjusted odds ratio: 11.5 for identifying HGD vs. LGD
2.11. Biliary Stricture Assessment - ERCP/Cholangioscopy
  • Visual exam alone has poor inter-observer agreement
  • Experts show good sensitivity but cannot agree on individual cases
  • AI goal: train to best experts in world, then scale
2.12. Mayo Biliary AI Model Development
  • Neil annotated 10,000 images
  • Categories: high quality benign/malignant/suspicious/low quality/uninformative
  • 900-frame analysis (~30 seconds of data)
  • Accuracy: ~91%, sensitivity: 93%, specificity lower than brush/forceps
2.13. Multicenter Validation - Biliary AI
  • 61 recorded videos from 3 sites (large for cholangioscopy)
  • AI sensitivity > brush cytology and forceps biopsies
  • Overall accuracy much higher than tissue sampling alone
2.14. Real-Time Implementation - User Interface
  • Running score classification (benign vs. malignant)
  • Waterfall plot visualization for cancer probability
  • Helps manage concerning strictures (even COVID cholangiopathy cases)
2.15. SMART AI Trial - Real-Time vs. Standard Care
  • Compared: cholangioscopy AI vs. brush cytology + transpapillary biopsies
  • Combination standard care performed much lower accuracy than AI alone
  • Secondary outcome: blinded human assessment comparison
2.16. Expert vs. Fellow Performance in SMART Trial
  • Experts: no better than untrained trainees on AI-analyzed videos
  • AI outperformed experienced and inexperienced endoscopists alike
2.17. Other International Groups - Biliary AI
  • Portugal/Spain combined centers developing systems
  • Carlos Orellana-Madrid (Ecuador) - different platform technology
  • Multiple exciting technologies in development
2.18. Future Needs for Biliary AI Integration
  • External validation on large datasets
  • Continuous feedback loops to improve algorithms
  • Real-time assessment development
  • Clearer regulatory pathways (FDA approval challenges)
3. AI in Inflammatory Bowel Disease Surveillance
3.1. Two Types of IBD Surveillance
  • Disease activity assessment (UC and Crohn's)
  • Cancer/dysplasia surveillance
3.2. Challenges with Current Endoscopic Scoring
  • Visual assessment is often arbitrary (Mayo, SES-CD scores)
  • Difficulty quantifying: erythema, ulceration, disease extent
  • Crohn's disease: heterogeneous and patchy --> quantification especially difficult
3.3. Spatial Endoscopic Scoring - UC
  • Maps disease severity to anatomical segments (~0.5cm increments)
  • Color coding: red = severe, green = normal, yellow/pink = intermediate
  • Detects subtle therapeutic responses that conventional scores miss
3.4. UNIFY Trial - Ustekinumab Phase III
  • Spatial mapping revealed differences within same Mayo score groups
  • Patients in clinical remission vs. symptomatic had different spatial patterns
  • Differences were statistically significant within Mayo groups
3.5. Clinical Implications of Spatial Scoring
  • Mayo score 3 patients showed variation in spatial distribution
  • This variability correlates with patient symptoms
  • More granular assessment changes how patients are phenotyped
3.6. Remaining Questions for Granular Scoring
  • How much change is clinically meaningful?
  • Thresholds for therapeutic response still being determined
  • Does this granularity matter if drugs can achieve endoscopic remission?
  • Resource investment vs. practical value debate ongoing
3.7. Crohn's Disease - Greater Challenge for AI
  • UC is homogeneous --> easier to assess
  • Crohn's: heterogeneous, patchy disease
  • SES-CD (2004) remains standard but has poor inter-rater agreement
3.8. SEMA Score (Simplified by Adler)
  • Even further simplified than SES-CD
  • Equal performance to SES-CD with less complexity
  • Still lacks granularity for precise disease assessment
3.9. AI Ulcer Segmentation - Real-Time
  • Blue = expert-drawn segmentation (7 physicians)
  • Yellow = AI-detected ulcer boundaries
  • Green = areas of agreement between human and AI
3.10. Ulcer Segmentation Accuracy
  • Overall AI-to-human agreement: >85%
  • When disagreement exists: coin flip whether AI or expert is more correct
  • Some cases where all 7 physicians missed ulcerations that AI detected
3.11. Spatial Mapping from Segmentation
  • Quantifies ulcer area directly (not guesstimating size/shape)
  • Allows cumulative disease burden calculation
  • More objective than subjective scoring systems
3.12. Cross-Sectional Imaging - CT/MR Enterography
  • Transmural healing increasingly important for Crohn's prognosis
  • Emerging true antifibrotics require accurate fibrosis detection
  • AI enables 3D spatial quantification of disease activity
3.13. Spatial Quantitation Methods
  • Automatic bowel segmentation in 3D
  • Curve planar reformation: straighten curved bowel for analysis
  • Extract geometric, texture, and image characteristic features
3.14. Mural Stratification Detection
  • MRI feature suggesting deep fibrosis
  • Pre-treatment prediction of biologic response vs. therapeutic failure
  • Could identify patients likely to require surgery
3.15. Complex Imaging Limitations
  • MR enterography: low resolution (512x512 pixels)
  • Cuts only 4-5mm apart
  • Source image quality limits AI capability
3.16. Intestinal Ultrasound - Emerging Opportunity
  • Operator-dependent imaging acquisition
  • AI quality depends on input image quality
  • Inter-operator variability remains challenge
3.17. Dysplasia Detection - "Seeing the Unseeable"
  • EndoBrain Group (2021): applied existing CAD models to IBD dysplasia
  • Proof of concept: AI can detect difficult flat dysplastic lesions
  • No specific IBD training initially but showed potential
3.18. Mayo Rochester - IBD-Specific CAD Model
  • Took conventional CAD (adenoma detection) + added IBD-specific data
  • Training on WLI and chromoendoscopy images
  • Now disambiguates: ulceration, inflammation, pseudopolyps vs. adenomas
3.19. IBD CAD Performance Comparison
  • Conventional CAD for IBD dysplasia: limited performance
  • With IBD-specific training data: significant improvement across features
  • Multiple feature types showed enhanced detection
3.20. Dysplasia Detection Challenges - Why Difficult
  • Rare condition --> limited training data
  • If humans cannot visually detect, AI also struggles to learn
  • Significant work still needed but promise exists
4. AI in Endoscopic Training and Competency
4.1. Variation in Endoscopy Quality
  • Wide variation in adenoma detection rates (ADR)
  • Measurement and feedback historically difficult
  • AI enables systematic performance tracking
4.2. Trainee Learning Curves - Individual Differences
  • No trainee is truly "average"
  • Some require >500 colonoscopies to achieve competency
  • Others competent by 150-200 procedures
  • Standard training assumes average learner
4.3. Current Training Supervision Gaps
  • Attending physicians often distracted (checking MyChart, etc.)
  • Third-year fellows: majority of withdrawal unsupervised
  • Critical teaching moments missed during procedure
4.4. ASG AI Task Force - Tenets of AI Education
  • Pre-adoption: understanding what AI is/how it works
  • Training integration into curricula
  • Autonomous implementation and proficiency assessment
  • Single-digit percentage of trainees understand these tenets
4.5. Feedback and Competency Measurement
  • Current: qualitative written evaluations + procedure volume only
  • Trainees need technique feedback, not just numbers
  • Few programs using formal skills assessment tools
4.6. Interactive Feedback Tool Development
  • Ingests any colonoscopy video automatically
  • Identifies relevant highlights (polyps, techniques)
  • Calculates withdrawal time, independent cecal intubation
  • Subtracts polypectomy time from withdrawal calculations
4.7. Visual Assessment Correlates with Performance
  • Watching 5 colonoscopies of high vs. low performer: can identify quality
  • Nurses recognize high/low performers without data
  • AI can systematically do this across entire practice
4.8. Annual Practice Review via AI
  • AI watches all procedure videos for entire year
  • Calculates: cecal intubations, polyp removals, cold snare rates
  • Identifies lower performers for targeted improvement
4.9. Trainee Tandem Study Findings
  • Trainees missed 55% of serrated polyps
  • Missed 31% of adenomatous polyps (2nd year > 3rd year)
  • Difficulty recognizing subtle lesions
4.10. Why Trainees Miss Polyps
  • 80% of misses: polyp on screen but trainee didn't recognize it
  • Only 20%: lesion not visible on screen at all
  • Recognition failure is main issue, not detection
4.11. AI as Teaching Tool vs. Hindrance
  • Hypothesis: AI highlights subtle lesions --> trainees learn patterns
  • Example: serrated polyp flagged by AI helps trainee recognize future cases
  • Creates feedback loop for learning
4.12. Ongoing Study - AI Use in Trainees
  • Tandem colonoscopies with/without AI for comparison
  • Qualitative interviews by Catherine Walsh (Canada)
  • Examines how trainees perceive AI impact on their learning
4.13. Descaling Concerns - Airline Parallel
  • Air France Flight 447 (2009): autopilot off, pilots couldn't recover manually
  • Pilots required to do manual flights to maintain skills
  • Question: should trainees practice without AI periodically?
4.14. Expert Panel Response to Descaling
  • Training programs should include withdrawals with AND without AI
  • Philosophical question: is practicing without HD scopes "upskilling"?
  • Trainees unlikely to practice without AI once in independent practice
4.15. Topol's Concern - Learning with AI Assistants
  • May never develop baseline skill set if always assisted
  • Counter: need certain competency level THEN use AI as enhancement
  • Parallel to driving with backup cameras --> still learn basic skills first
4.16. Education Integration - Essay Analogy
  • Universities now require AI-written applications
  • Then ask applicants to critique AI output and improve prompts
  • GI training could require trainees explain why AI failed in cases
4.17. Geographic/Regional Training Data Concerns
  • Systems trained in referral centers may not generalize
  • Example: Barrett's AI developed where dysplasia more common
  • Applied in regions with different disease prevalence
4.18. External Validation Requirements
  • FDA approval process requires training in diverse settings
  • Community centers must be included, not just academic referral sites
  • New products can use "substantially equivalent" pathway (less rigorous)
4.19. Ground Truth and Data Quality
  • Models depend on quality of input data and annotations
  • AI scientists sometimes say: "just give me 10,000 videos, I'll figure it out"
  • Still requires expert annotation for meaningful training
4.20. Multimodal Integration Importance
  • Must link AI to endoscopic records, not just images alone
  • Example: cholangioscopy AI should include CA19-9, patient age/risk factors
  • Prevents inappropriate predictions based on visual data alone
5. Audience Q&A: Key Themes and Debates
5.1. Descaling vs. Upskilling Debate
  • Topol concern: learning with AI assistants may eliminate baseline skills
  • Counterpoint: trainees must develop competency THEN use AI as enhancement
  • Driving analogy: backup cameras don't prevent learning to drive
5.2. Zebra Detection Concern
  • If AI only trained on common patterns, rare conditions may be missed
  • Example: Mickey Mouse hidden in lung CT - nobody found it (not trained for it)
  • AI will never find what it wasn't trained to recognize
5.3. Natural Language Processing for Surveillance
  • AI reviews endoscopy + pathology reports automatically
  • Generates surveillance recommendations without human review
  • ~90% accuracy compared to guidelines
5.4. Individualized Physician Performance Feedback
  • AI could stratify surveillance intervals based on individual ADR
  • Low performers: shorter intervals, high performers: longer intervals
  • Could customize recommendations per physician
5.5. User Adoption Challenges - Ellen Barkin RCT
  • Large RCT: AI polyp detector randomized by room (AI on vs. off)
  • 30-40% of physicians turned machine off despite trial participation
  • Some practitioners simply don't want AI assistance
5.6. Technology vs. Training Improvements
  • ADR improvement came from HD scopes, not just AI alone
  • AI is one component of many needed for quality improvement
  • Technology + training + feedback systems all important
5.7. Ground Truth Requirements for AI Development
  • Still need expert annotation despite massive data availability
  • Classical scientific methodology remains important
  • "Magical thinking" that AI doesn't need good data is problematic
5.8. Spatial Biology and New Imaging Modalities
  • Where humans cannot process data (too complex for visual cortex)
  • AI may identify patterns invisible to human perception
  • Emerging imaging types offer opportunities beyond traditional annotation
5.9. Fibrosis Detection - Exception to Annotation Rule
  • Radiologists cannot distinguish inflammation vs. fibrosis on imaging
  • AI with texture analysis can detect what experts cannot see
  • This is where "unseenable" concept applies most clearly
5.10. Future Directions - Consensus Points
  • External validation on large diverse datasets needed
  • Continuous feedback loops to improve algorithms
  • Regulatory pathways need clarification for FDA approval
  • Training programs must integrate AI education
