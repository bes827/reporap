Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease and Reflux-like Phenotypes: Using Lyon Consensus 2.0 to Guide Clinical Approach
  • Source: Journal of Neurogastroenterology and Motility, July 2026
  • Topic: Clinical application of Montreal, Lyon 2.0, and Rome V criteria for phenotyping GERD and related disorders.
  • Author(s): Schroeder, M. K., and Gyawali, C. P.
1. Overview of Diagnostic Frameworks
  • Montreal Consensus: Introduced symptom-based definition; defined mucosal injury phenotypes.
  • Lyon Consensus (2.0): Focuses on objective testing to define conclusive GERD vs. absence of GERD.
  • Rome V Criteria: Defines Esophageal Disorders of Gut–Brain Interaction (E-DGBI); requires exclusion of GERD via Lyon criteria.
2. Troublesome Symptoms & Empiric Therapy
2.1. Typical Symptoms (Heartburn, Acid Regurgitation, Chest Pain)
  • PPI Response:
  • Heartburn: >70% respond; placebo response up to 25%.
  • Regurgitation: ~50% improve; often persists despite heartburn relief.
  • Esophageal chest pain: 56-85% show $\ge 50\%$ improvement (if objective GERD present).
  • Diagnostic Value: PPI responsiveness has sensitivity 84% and specificity 76% for chest pain, but low specificity (54%) overall.
  • False Positives: In endoscopy-negative heartburn patients with $\ge 75\%$ PPI improvement, only 55% had abnormal reflux burden; others had Reflux Hypersensitivity (RH) (26%) or Functional Heartburn (FH) (19%).
2.2. Atypical & Laryngopharyngeal Symptoms (LPS)
  • LPS Profile: Includes chronic cough, hoarse voice, throat clearing, sore throat, excessive phlegm.
  • PPI Efficacy: Poor response; no significant benefit over placebo in undifferentiated LPS or chronic cough.
  • Clinical Guidance: Lyon 2.0 and San Diego Consensus recommend upfront objective testing before anti-reflux therapy for isolated LPS.
  • Other Atypical Symptoms:
  • Globus: Negatively associated with conclusive GERD (OR 0.5 [0.30–0.86]).
  • Belching: May be reflux-related or behavioral; requires pH-impedance monitoring.
  • Nausea/Dyspepsia/Dysphagia: Very low likelihood of objective GERD.
3. Diagnostic Evaluation Tools
3.1. Upper Endoscopy (EGD)
  • Yield: Erosive esophagitis found in only 1/3 of treatment-naïve patients; <10% in those already on PPIs.
  • Healing Rates: $\ge 70\%$ heal with PPIs; $\ge 90\%$ heal with potassium competitive acid blockers (P-CABs).
  • Protocol: Recommended off therapy for 2-4 weeks if diagnosis is uncertain.
3.2. Ambulatory Reflux Monitoring
  • Wireless pH Monitoring: Preferred for typical symptoms/negative endoscopy; overcomes day-to-day variability (up to 96 hours).
  • pH-Impedance Monitoring:
  • Higher sensitivity for weakly acidic/non-acidic reflux.
  • Provides Mean Nocturnal Baseline Impedance (MNBI) as a marker of mucosal integrity.
  • High-Resolution Manometry (HRM):
  • Assesses peristalsis and EGJ morphology.
  • Essential pre-operative tool; identifies achalasia/obstructive disorders in ~3% of surgical candidates.
3.3. Barium Esophagography
  • Accuracy: Low sensitivity (37.6%) and specificity (71.6%) for conclusive GERD.
  • Primary Use: Characterizing EGJ morphology or identifying obstructive processes.
4. Mucosal Injury Phenotypes (Conclusive GERD)
4.1. Erosive Esophagitis
  • LA Classification: Higher grades $\rightarrow$ $\uparrow$ Acid Exposure Time (AET), $\uparrow$ symptom severity, $\uparrow$ PPI response.
  • Lyon 2.0 Update: LA Grade B is now considered conclusive GERD (similar reflux burden to Grade C).
4.2. Peptic Strictures & Barrett’s Esophagus (BE)
  • Peptic Strictures: Typically short (<2 cm); PPIs superior to H2 blockers for resolution and preventing recurrence.
  • Barrett’s Esophagus:
  • Requires biopsy-proven intestinal metaplasia.
  • Long-segment BE (>3 cm) $\rightarrow$ $\uparrow$ AET, $\uparrow$ total reflux episodes, $\downarrow$ acid clearance time.
  • Ultrashort segment BE (<1 cm): Controversial; one study found 8.4% developed definitive dysplasia.
5. Abnormal Reflux Burden Phenotypes (NERD)
5.1. Acid Exposure Time (AET) Thresholds
  • Pathologic: $\ge 6\%$ (Western populations); $\ge 4\%$ (Asian populations).
  • Physiologic: $< 4\%$.
  • Inconclusive: $4-6\%$ (requires supportive data).
5.2. Supportive Metrics for Inconclusive AET ($4-6\%$)
  • Reflux Episodes: $\ge 80$ total episodes off acid suppression $\rightarrow$ strong evidence for escalating management.
  • MNBI: Low MNBI (<1500 \Omega) suggests mucosal injury even if AET is borderline.
  • Reflux Symptom Association (RSA): Uses symptom association probability >95% and symptom index >50%.
6. Normal Reflux Burden & Behavioral Phenotypes
6.1. Pure E-DGBI (Rome V)
  • Criteria: Heartburn/chest pain $\ge 6$ months + normal endoscopy + physiologic AET (<4%) + no RSA.
  • Subtypes:
  • Functional Heartburn (FH) / Functional Chest Pain.
  • Reflux Hypersensitivity (RH): Diagnosed if RSA is present despite physiologic AET.
6.2. Behavioral Syndromes
  • Supragastric Belching (SGB): Air pushed into esophagus and expelled without reaching stomach; $\ge 13$ episodes/24h considered abnormal.
  • Rumination Syndrome: Effortless regurgitation of recent food; occurs post-prandially; stops when contents become acidic.
  • Management: Behavioral therapy, diaphragmatic breathing, neuromodulators (Anti-reflux surgery is contraindicated for isolated SGB).
7. Esophageal Hypervigilance
  • Definition: Cognitive-affective pattern where patients are hyperaware of esophageal sensations $\rightarrow$ catastrophic thinking $\rightarrow$ disproportionate behavioral response.
  • Impact: Predicts symptom severity regardless of actual acid burden; correlates with $\downarrow$ quality of life.
  • Measurement: Esophageal Hypervigilance and Anxiety Scale (EHAS) (15-item tool).
  • Clinical Significance: Independent predictor of pre-operative symptom severity and post-operative improvement.
