THEME 1: Real-Time Visual Detection During Procedures

AI that highlights suspicious areas on the screen while the endoscopist is performing the procedure — "green boxes" and heat maps.

Slide 1: CADe for Colorectal Polyps — The Evidence Base

What it does: Detects polyps in real time during colonoscopy, alerting the endoscopist with visual cues.

RCT evidence (controlled settings):

  • ~48 randomized controlled trials, over 40,000 patients: CADe increases adenoma detection rate (ADR) by ~20%, decreases adenoma miss rate by ~50% (2530, lines 47-48)
  • Benefits seen across ALL experience levels — not just low-performing endoscopists (2530, lines 47-48)

Real-world pragmatic evidence (the nuance):

  • Multicenter concealed RCT (1,744 patients, 3 Quebec centers): In intention-to-treat analysis, CADe availability showed NO significant ADR improvement. Per-protocol (actual users) showed >5% ADR gain (2185, lines 164-179)
  • Real-world CADe utilization was only 57.8% (vs. planned 70%), with center-level variation from 40-70% (2185, lines 167-169)
  • Key finding: Advanced lesion detection was significant in BOTH ITT and per-protocol — CADe helps even at system level for clinically important lesions (2185, lines 169-170)

Population-level signal:

  • National propensity-matched study of 3,000,000 colonoscopies (TriNetX): AI era associated with 97% increase in adenoma detection, 48% increase in advanced adenoma detection, and 47% reduction in interval colorectal cancer (RR 0.53) (2185, lines 52-54)
  • Caveat: Era comparison, not direct AI vs. no-AI; other quality improvements occurred simultaneously (2185, lines 59-60)

Source: 2530 - AI In Your Endoscopy Practice | Lines: 47-48
Source: 2185 - AI Applications in Colorectal Diseases | Lines: 52-64, 164-179

Slide 2: CADe for Barrett's Esophagus — The "Democratizer"

What it does: Highlights suspicious mucosal areas in the Barrett's segment that warrant targeted biopsy.

The democratization effect:

  • General gastroenterologists with AI achieved detection rates matching Barrett's experts (95-96% sensitivity/specificity) — "You all of a sudden become an expert" (5100, lines 49-51)

DODGE CADe system (real-time in vivo):

  • One-pass endoscopy with AI: 100% sensitivity vs. 53% without AI (5100, lines 53-54)
  • Every-2-cm assessment: 100% sensitivity, 73% specificity (5100, lines 53-54)
  • Heat map visualization highlights flat lesions and subtle erythematous areas missed without AI (5100, lines 55-57)

Large prospective clinical study (200 patients, 3 Dutch expert centers):

  • CADe detected 89% of visible lesions identified by experts; 0.6 false positives per patient (one extra biopsy every 2 patients) (5255, lines 41-47)
  • Critical methodological lesson: For low-prevalence diseases, primary endpoint should be endoscopic detection of visually suspicious lesions — NOT histology. Histology undermines true detection performance (biopsy sampling error, pathologist variability) and requires impractically large sample sizes (5255, lines 49-53)

Slide 4: Cholangioscopy AI for Biliary Strictures

What it does: Classifies indeterminate biliary strictures as benign vs. malignant in real time during cholangioscopy.

Performance:

  • Mayo Clinic: 10,000 annotated images trained a model achieving 91% accuracy (93% sensitivity) for benign vs. malignant classification (5100, lines 129-130)
  • Multicenter validation (61 videos): AI accuracy significantly higher than brush cytology or forceps biopsies alone (5100, lines 131-134)
  • SMART AI trial (real-time): AI accuracy superior to COMBINATION of brush cytology + transpapillary forceps biopsies (5100, lines 139-142)
  • Striking finding: Experts were no better than untrained trainees on visual assessment alone — the human eye cannot reliably distinguish benign from malignant strictures visually (5100, lines 139-142)

THEME 2: Optical Diagnosis / CADEX — "Resect and Discard"

AI that classifies a lesion's histology in real time, enabling immediate clinical decisions without waiting for pathology.

Slide 5: CADEX for Colorectal Polyps

What it does: Classifies a polyp as hyperplastic, adenomatous, or cancerous during the procedure — enabling "resect and discard" for benign-appearing diminutive polyps.

Performance:

  • CADEX systems achieve 85-90% accuracy matching expert-level optical diagnosis (2530, lines 51)
  • Multiple FDA-cleared and CE-marked systems available; U.S. adoption still emerging (2530, lines 51-52)

Clinical benefits:

  • Immediate resect-and-discard decisions for diminutive rectosigmoid polyps (2530, lines 53-54)
  • Reduced pathology costs, decreased unnecessary polypectomies, faster workflow (2530, lines 53-54)
  • Can inform patients day-of-colonoscopy about surveillance intervals without waiting for histopathology (2530, lines 54)

THEME 3: Quantitative Assessment Beyond Categorical Scoring

AI that provides continuous, granular measurements where human assessment relies on coarse categorical buckets — revealing information lost in traditional scoring.

Slide 6: AI Spatial Scoring in IBD — Beyond Mayo Scores

What it does: Maps disease severity at half-centimeter increments along the colon, capturing spatial variability that a single Mayo endoscopic score erases.

Key finding from the UNIFY trial (ustekinumab Phase III):

  • Within identical Mayo endoscopic score groups, AI spatial scores revealed statistically significant differences that correlated with patient symptoms (5100, lines 171-176)
  • "More granularity actually matters" — two patients both classified as Mayo 3 can have vastly different cumulative disease burden (5100, lines 173-174)

Why this matters: Conventional scoring systems collapse rich spatial information into a single number. AI preserves that granularity, enabling better treatment response assessment and trial endpoints (5100, lines 167-175)

THEME 4: Acute Event Risk Prediction from Clinical Data

Machine learning models trained on structured EHR data (labs, vitals, demographics) to predict near-term adverse outcomes — outperforming traditional clinical scores.

Slide 9: Variceal Hemorrhage — 6-Week Mortality and Rebleeding

Model: Multimodal XGBoost (MIMIC-IV, 2008-2022)

Performance:

  • AUROC 0.89 for 6-week mortality, vs. MELD-Na at 0.78 (5255, lines 65-84)
  • AUROC 0.80 for 6-week rebleeding, vs. MELD-Na at 0.70 (5255, lines 79-81)

What makes it different from existing scores:

  • Integrates structured clinical data (labs, vitals, transfusions) + endoscopic free text extracted via NLP (variceal size, location, active spurting) (5255, lines 73-78)
  • Existing scores (MELD, CHLPU, Rockall, Glasgow) each miss a piece: hemodynamics, endoscopy severity, or early transfusion burden (5255, lines 67-68)

Top predictors (SHAP analysis): Lactate/creatinine (hepatorenal syndrome), hemodynamic instability, active spurting on EGD, early transfusion requirement (5255, lines 81-84)

Slide 10: Cirrhosis — 90-Day Transplant-Free Survival

Slide 11: Acute Pancreatitis — 30-Day Unplanned Readmission

Model: XGBoost (MIMIC-IV, 4,362 adults)

Performance:

  • AUROC 0.82 (vs. logistic regression 0.71, BISAP+LOS 0.66); calibration: 0.98 (2110, lines 27-30)

Other key predictors: CRP (persistent inflammation), albumin (nutritional reserve), LOS, peripancreatic fluid collection, prior hospitalization within 6 months (2110, lines 33-35)

Simplified bedside score: 6-variable version achieved AUROC 0.78; decision curve analysis showed net benefit across 10-50% risk thresholds (2110, lines 35-39)

Methodological innovation: Multiple instance learning handles the challenge that not every pixel on a slide contributes to progression risk — only specific "bags" of patches matter (5255, lines 169-170)

Slide 14: Blood-Based Biomarkers for Esophageal Cancer — Detection Years in Advance

Study: UK Biobank proteomic cohort (50,000 patients) + independent validation cohort

Slide 15: Multi-Organ Radiomics for Early Pancreatic Cancer Detection

What it does: Detects subtle pancreatic cancer features on CT scans acquired 6-24 months before clinical diagnosis.

Key methodological insight — multi-organ approach:

  • Multi-organ radiomics (pancreas + liver + body composition): AUROC 0.72 vs. pancreas-only at 0.69 (2110, lines 237-238)
  • In the 6-24 month window, feature importance was spread across pancreas shape/texture, liver diameter, and visceral/subcutaneous adipose tissue (2110, lines 245-247)
  • Risk scores showed significant increasing trend as scans approached clinical diagnosis, even when CTs appeared normal (2110, lines 239-241)

CT-based early recognition (EfficientNet-B4 "Spark" model):

  • 99% accuracy, AUROC near 1.0 for detecting subtle early pancreatic cancer features (2110, lines 198-206)

  • 38% of stage IV pancreatic cancer patients had subtle features on prior CT scans that were missed by human readers (2110, lines 189-191)

THEME 7: Treatment Optimization and Counterfactual Analysis

AI that answers "what if" — simulating alternative treatment paths to identify the optimal strategy for each patient.

Slide 17: HCC Treatment Optimization — The "What If" Simulator

Model: Random forest (~7,000 HCC patients from 2 Italian registries; externally validated on Japanese cohort)

Performance:

  • AUROC: 83% at 1 year, 76% at 5 years (internal); 72% at 5 years (external Japanese validation) (4480, lines 114-115)

Counterfactual analysis:

  • At 5 years, 55% of patients would be reallocated to different treatments under AI optimization (4480, lines 117-120)
  • Liver transplantation was optimal for 22% of patients but only delivered in 1.5% of cases (4480, lines 117-120)
  • Median predicted survival gain: 2% at 1 year, up to 24% at 5 years if AI-optimized treatment was followed (4480, lines 121-122)

Deployment: "Augmented multidisciplinary meeting tool" — web-based application for tumor board use (4480, lines 123-125)

THEME 9: Histopathology — Unsupervised and Self-Supervised Discovery

AI that finds patterns without human labels — discovering novel disease subtypes and biological signals that humans haven't categorized.

Slide 21: Achalasia Subtyping via Hierarchical Clustering

What it does: Identifies distinct histopathological subtypes of achalasia without pre-defined labels.

Findings (100 POEM patients, Shenzhen University):

  • 4 distinct subtypes identified by hierarchical clustering (2105, lines 100-117):
    • Subtype 1 (n=59): Classic neuron loss — prominent reduction of nitrergic neurons, higher IRP
    • Subtype 2 (n=8): Neuron-preserved/role injury — higher total neurons, lower IRP
    • Subtype 3: Immune-mediated — high T cells, B cells, macrophages
    • Subtype 4 (n=29): Coagulation/vascular — high thrombus burden, higher IRP

Why it matters: Histology-based subtyping for understanding disease mechanisms and guiding personalized treatment — a pattern humans had not systematically categorized (2105, lines 114-117)

Source: 2105 - Esophageal Motility Disorders | Lines: 100-117

Slide 22: Self-Supervised Feature Learning for Barrett's Dysplasia

Method:

  • Unsupervised model trained to replicate input images (self-supervision), learning patterns without human labels (5255, lines 213-214)
  • Embedding space projected to 2D and validated by a GI pathologist — distinct clusters emerged for intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, submucosal gland (5255, lines 217-218)
  • These embeddings then fed into a supervised classifier for low-grade vs. high-grade dysplasia (5255, lines 218-219)

Key insight: Self-supervised learning captures biological structure that labeled training alone might miss — the unsupervised model "sees" what a pathologist would recognize, without being told what to look for (5255, lines 213-218)

THEME 14: The Hawthorne Effect and Concealed Trials

The methodological challenge of measuring real-world AI performance when knowing you're being evaluated changes behavior.

Slide 32: The Barkin Trial — When AI Availability Doesn't Improve Outcomes in ITT

Design: Fully concealed multicenter RCT (1,744 patients, 3 Quebec academic centers) — endoscopists and staff were UNAWARE of the trial.

Results:

  • When CADe was available but not used, ADR did NOT improve in intention-to-treat analysis (2185, lines 164-179)
  • Per-protocol analysis (actual users): >5% ADR improvement (2185, lines 169-170)
  • Real-world utilization: only 57.8% (vs. planned 70%) (2185, lines 167-169)

The lesson:

  • "Clinical AI succeeds or fails at the level of workflow integration and clinician adoption, rather than solely at the level of algorithm performance" (2185, lines 172-173)
  • This concealed RCT design is proposed as a "reproducible and rigorous framework for AI decision support tools" (2185, lines 179-180)

Slide 41: Algorithmic Bias and Fairness

The problem:

  • "If the data contains bias, the algorithm is going to reinforce existing disparities" — Dr. Leggett (2530, lines 39-40)
  • Models trained in high-prevalence referral centers may fail in community practice (5100, lines 309-312)
  • Data shift: Algorithm designed for one population may degrade in another (2530, lines 40-41)

Mitigation strategies:

  • Multi-center, multi-continent data collection (pancreatic cyst AI: 40 centers, 5 continents) (2110, lines 73-74)
  • Interoperability features for different image sizes/brands (2110, lines 115-119)
  • AAMC best practices: protect against bias, provide notice and explanation, incorporate human judgment (2035, lines 86-91)

The "zebra" problem: AI trained on common presentations may miss rare conditions (5100, lines 291-294)

Slide 45: Red Flags for Overhyped Tools

Red Flag 1: No external validation — internal AUROCs of 0.99+ that drop to 0.95 externally (2185, lines 122-123)

Red Flag 2: Claims that ignore the Hawthorne effect — be skeptical of studies where endoscopists knew they were being evaluated (2185, lines 164-179)

Red Flag 3: "Black box" models without explainability — SHAP values and attention maps should be standard (2110, lines 31-35; 4480, lines 298-311)

Red Flag 4: Training data that doesn't match your population — ask about disease prevalence in training set (5100, lines 309-312)

Red Flag 5: Vague "AI-powered" claims without specifics — distinguish CADe, CADEX, CAQ, and documentation (2530, lines 44-79)

Slide 47: Final Take-Home Messages

For the skeptic:

  • AI is not replacing you. It is becoming your most consistent colleague — tireless, objective, and improving with every case (5100, lines 46-47)
  • The question is no longer "if" but "how" (2530, lines 211-213)

For the early adopter:

  • Demand explainability: SHAP values, attention maps, confidence scores should accompany every prediction (2110, lines 31-35)
  • Verify before you trust: External validation matters more than internal AUROC (2185, lines 122-123)

For the trainee:

  • Learn basics first, then integrate AI — don't start with green boxes on day one (2530, lines 233-235)
  • Your most valuable future skill is JUDGMENT — knowing when to trust and when to override (2530, lines 316-318)

For the administrator:

  • Pick problems first, vendors second. Build architecture that supports layering of future tools (2530, lines 269-274)
  • Data governance and patient consent are not optional — they are foundational (2530, lines 137-139)

The bottom line:

"AI does not change what a good report needs to contain. But implemented well, AI might be able to change how hard it is to get there consistently." — Dr. Silverman (2530, lines 150-151)
