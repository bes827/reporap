Artificial Intelligence and Precision Education in Gastroenterology
  • Source: DDW (Digestive Disease Week) Session
  • Topic: AI applications in GI training, adenoma detection, procedural assessment, and precision medical education
  • Author(s): Elizabeth Stadum, Varun Perinathan, Lauren Sanchez, Shelley Malik, Justin Sewell (et al.)
1. CADDY Systems and Adenoma Detection Rates
1.1 Background on Computer-Aided Detection (CADe)
  • High-quality colonoscopy essential for CRC prevention
  • ADR --> key quality metric in colorectal cancer prevention
  • CADe systems shown to ↑ adenoma detection rates in multiple studies
  • Gap: how CADe impacts different trainee experience levels remains uncharacterized
1.2 Prior Study Findings
  • Beginner endoscopists: +19% gross gains in ADR using CADe vs. not
  • Intermediate providers: ~+10% gains in ADR utilizing CADe
  • Most junior endoscopists (excluding one) had ≥10% ADR gains with CADe
  • AI-assisted colonoscopy showed significant ADR improvements
  • No statistically significant gains in advanced adenomas or sessile lesions regardless of experience
1.3 Study Design (Multicentric Retrospective)
  • Conducted at two large academic training centers in Alabama (UAB + USA)
  • Retrospective observational study using Provation database
  • Time period: 2022–2025
  • Inclusion criteria: average-risk screening colonoscopies only
  • Exclusion criteria: diagnostic procedures, attending-only performed cases
1.4 Cohort Characteristics
  • Total analyzed: >4,800 colonoscopies
  • CADe group: ~2,200 eligible
  • Non-CADe group: ~1,600 eligible
  • Gender distribution: 55% female / 45% male
  • Mean patient age: 57.4 years
  • Broad provider representation from PGY-1 fellows to attendings
1.5 Outcome Metrics
  • Primary outcome: combined ADR + SDR
  • Secondary outcomes:
  • ADR alone
  • SSP (sessile serrated polyp) detection
  • Withdrawal time
  • Average combined ADR/SDR: 56.5%
  • Mean withdrawal time: 13.1 minutes
  • Prep quality: 85%
1.6 Results by Provider Experience Level
  • First-year fellows: +10.3% gross gains in combined outcomes with CADe
  • Attendings: almost +15% gains in combined outcomes
  • Second-year fellows: moderate gains
  • Third-year fellows: minimal gains from CADe
1.7 Subgroup Analysis Findings
  • ADR data matches combined outcome patterns
  • SDR increases across all experience levels: +2.5% to +7.5%
  • First-year fellows and attendings showed most significant gains
1.8 Study Limitations
  • Retrospective, observational design
  • Only two centers in Alabama
  • Variable attending supervision across PGY levels
  • Outcomes limited to detection rates (no long-term clinical endpoints)
1.9 Key Implications
  • Supports integration of CADe into fellowship training centers
  • Benefits both junior trainees and experienced providers
  • Attendings trained without CADe may benefit most from re-exposure
2. AI-Based Phase Recognition in Per Oral Endoscopic Myotomy (POEM)
2.1 Background on POEM
  • POEM --> preferred minimally invasive treatment for achalasia
  • Clinical success exceeding 90%
  • Technically demanding with six distinct phases
  • Current training relies on subjective assessment
  • No standardized competency benchmarks exist
2.2 The Six Phases of POEM

1. Submucosal injection

2. Mucosal incision

3. Submucosal entry

4. Submucosal tunneling

5. Myotomy of circular muscle fibers

6. Closure of mucosal incision

2.3 Study Design and Methods
  • Multi-center retrospective study
  • 140 complete POEM procedure videos from two institutions (Mayo Clinic + AdventHealth Orlando)
  • Three POEM-trained endoscopists manually annotated every frame into six phases
  • Deep visual embeddings extracted using Genesis (endoscopy-specific foundation model)
  • Sliding window strategy applied for temporal smoothing
  • Linear classifier trained on 80/10/10 split
  • Hyperparameters optimized via grid search
2.4 AI Pipeline Architecture
  • Video acquisition --> frame extraction at 5 frames per second
  • Manual annotation by experts
  • Genesis model generates rich image embeddings
  • Temporal smoothing via sliding window approach
  • Linear classifier for phase prediction
  • Deployable on any clinical endoscopy platform
2.5 Model Performance Results
  • Overall accuracy: 94.2% across 87,915 independent test frames
  • Weighted F1 score: 0.942
  • Clinically critical phases showed excellent performance:
  • Myotomy: F1 = 0.966
  • Submucosal tunneling: F1 = 0.957
  • Closure: F1 = 0.943
2.6 Challenging Phase: Submucosal Entry
  • F1 score only 0.40
  • Most challenging phase due to:
  • Brief time spent in this phase
  • Visual similarity to submucosal tunneling
  • Main confusion: overlap with adjacent phases
2.7 ROC Analysis Results
  • Myotomy, submucosal tunneling, closure: AUC approaching 1.0
  • Submucosal entry (most challenging): AUC = 0.827 (well above random)
  • All phases substantially outperformed chance across classification thresholds
2.8 Temporal Consistency Analysis
  • Straightforward cases: near-perfect concordance between AI and ground truth
  • Complex cases with challenging transitions: model maintained strong temporal consistency
  • Accurately tracked procedural progression throughout
2.9 Practical Applications Identified
  • Training assessment: competency metrics for trainees over time
  • Quality improvement: identifying phases where excessive time may increase complications (e.g., capnoperitoneum)
  • Future consideration: splitting tunneling phase into sub-phases (pre-LES vs. high-pressure zone)
2.10 Future Directions
  • Prospective validation planned
  • Expansion to other third-space procedures (e.g., ESD)
  • Integration into endoscopy platform for real-time support
  • Training and quality improvement applications
3. AI Adaptive Self-Assessment Tool for GI Fellows
3.1 Program Definition: GIESAP (GI Endoscopy Self-Assessment Program)
  • Stream --> 5-week period with 2–3 case-based questions every other day
  • Each stream: unique bank of questions
  • Questions --> training level-specific (PGY-1, PGY-2, PGY-3)
  • Developed using validated surgery education program
  • Focus: continued knowledge mastery, retention, and professional development
3.2 Question Format
  • Case-based scenario at top
  • Multiple choice options below
  • Immediate reveal of correct response after selection
  • Detailed explanation provided:
  • Why correct answer is correct
  • High-yield information on diagnosis
  • Analysis of incorrect answers with high-yield info
3.3 Visual Learning Integration
  • Partnership with ASGE provided access to:
  • High-quality endoscopic images
  • Videos integrated into answer explanations
  • Both visual and text-based learning supported
3.4 Proficiency Definition
  • Proficiency = answering question correctly twice in a row
  • Decreases likelihood of correct answer by chance
  • ↑ likelihood of true knowledge acquisition
3.5 Adult Learning Theory Foundation
  • Forgetting curve (Ebbinghaus, late 1800s):
  • Within first 24 hours: lose 50–75% of learned information
  • Followed by exponential decay
  • Spaced repetition: repeated exposure over interval periods
  • Each repeat --> ↓ loss of information
  • Decreases forgetting curve
3.6 Pilot Study (Single Center)
  • 15 GI fellows across all training years
  • Single tertiary medical center
  • Enrolled in streams 1 and 2 (two 5-week periods)
  • Iterative neural network evaluated proficiency in real time
  • Adjusted topic, timing, frequency of questions based on responses
3.7 Pilot Results
  • Clear improvement in proficiency across all training years post-tool use
  • Fellows with higher engagement showed:
  • Greater gains in proficiency
  • Higher post-intervention scores
  • Association between engagement and educational benefit
3.8 Topic-Specific Analysis (Pilot)
  • Largest proficiency gains in topics with lowest baseline scores:
  • CR (colorec tal) esophagus
  • Bariatric endoscopy
  • Stomach section
  • Notable topics often absent from traditional board prep:
  • Bariatric endoscopy
  • Professional development
  • Endohepatology
  • Patient prep
3.9 Expansion Study (Multi-Center)
  • >15 medical centers and >190 fellows
  • Enrolled in streams 1 through 6
  • Methodology unchanged
  • Iterative neural network adjusted lesson plans targeting weak areas
3.10 Expanded Results
  • Proficiency improvements across all training years (including advanced fellows)
  • Durable and replicable results across diverse national sample
  • First-year fellows in stream 1 → second-year by stream 6 (received more advanced questions)
3.11 Heat Map Analysis (Expanded)
  • Improvement across every topic following tool utilization
  • Largest gains in initially lowest-scoring topics:
  • Liver section: doubled following tool use
  • Small bowel: ~20–30% ↑ post-tool
  • Colorectal: ~20–30% ↑ post-tool
3.12 Program Benefits
  • Rapid integration of new guidelines
  • Access to information not yet in traditional board prep material
  • Critical for continued clinical success
3.13 Leadership and Program Analytics
  • Data available to program leadership for:
  • Tailored, data-driven curriculum changes
  • Monitoring mastery
  • Tracking knowledge acquisition
  • Identifying weak areas in overall curriculum
3.14 Future: Generative AI Integration
  • Transitioning from predictive to generative AI model
  • Utilizing existing training material
  • Real-time content creation via "Case-based e-platform Generative GPT"
  • Users can:
  • Disassemble/reassemble previous material
  • Generate scenario-based questions
  • Access videos and verbal high-yield information
  • Equivalent of asking for current guidelines + scenarios + questions on any topic
3.15 Summary Features
  • Scenario-based testing
  • Science-backed learning algorithms for spaced repetition
  • Leaderboards and recognition for engagement
  • Performance readiness analytics dashboards
  • Manager coaching dashboards
4. Large Language Models in Clinical Decision Making (UGI Bleeding)
4.1 Study Background and Gaps
  • LLMs posited to support clinicians' information needs and decision-making
  • Real-world impacts on clinical settings and medical education remain unclear
4.2 Prior Study Reference (Sunny Chung, Dennis Chung Team)
  • Developed CDSS with customized LLM for UGI bleeding management
  • Called GutGPT (customized LLM)
  • Findings:
  • Users with LLM-enhanced CDSS had ↑ effort expectancy (ease of use)
  • No significant difference in intention
  • Limitation: did not examine knowledge or confidence in UGI bleeding management
4.3 Current Study Objectives
  • Investigate effect of AI CDSS with vs. without GutGPT on:
  • Users' knowledge of UGI bleeding management
  • Confidence in that knowledge
  • Adoption propensity of AI CDSS for UGI bleeding
4.4 Study Extension by Experience Level
  • Extended to examine experience level effects across:
  • Physicians (PGY-2 through senior consultants)
  • Medical students
4.5 AI Tool Components
4.5.1 Machine Learning Risk Prediction Dashboard
  • Patient-specific risk scores: 0 to 1 (higher = higher risk)
  • Shows number of similar patients requiring hospital-based intervention
  • Charts indicating features contributing to risk prediction
4.5.2 GutGPT (Customized LLM)
  • Layered on top of ML dashboard
  • Similar interface to ChatGPT
  • Trained on UGI bleeding guidelines
  • Responses supported with guideline references
4.6 Study Design (PICO Format)
  • Population: Medical students, physicians PGY-2 through senior consultants in UGI-facing specialties
  • Intervention arm: ML dashboard + GutGPT (LLM-enhanced)
  • Comparator arm: ML dashboard only (without LLM)
4.7 Outcome Variables
  • Adoption propensity: AI CDSS use intention
  • Explainability perception (understanding factors/process behind recommendations)
  • Knowledge in UGI bleeding management
  • Confidence in knowledge
4.8 Simulation Session Procedure

1. Pre-simulation survey completion

2. Randomization to GutGPT+dashboard OR dashboard-only arm

3. Simulation session with 5 UGI bleeding scenarios

4. First three scenarios: admittance + scope decisions

5. Post-survey phase 1

6. Two additional scenarios: care management (medications, blood transfusion)

7. Post-survey phase 2

8. One-on-one interview

4.9 Simulation Environment
  • Mimicked emergency ward setting realistically
  • Participants equipped with:
  • Electronic medical records (patient-specific)
  • AI tool (dashboard ± GutGPT)
  • Research team member acted as simulated patient
  • Participants could do history taking
  • Decisions made at end of each scenario
4.10 Current Enrollment Status
  • Target: 100 participants
  • Enrolled so far: 80 (ongoing study)
  • 42 physicians
  • 38 students
  • 42 in GutGPT arm
  • 38 in dashboard-only arm
4.11 Statistical Analysis Method
  • Repeated measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA)
  • Assessed impact of pre/post simulation on outcomes
  • Examined arm effects and experience level effects
4.12 Knowledge and Confidence Results
  • Knowledge ↑ for both arms
  • Confidence ↑ for both arms
  • Increase in knowledge more prominent in GutGPT arm (not statistically significant)
  • Knowledge/confidence gains driven mostly by medical students
  • Physicians showed ↑ confidence after simulation
4.13 Adoption Propensity Results (Differential by Arm and Experience)
4.13.1 AI CDSS Use Intention
  • Marginally ↑ only among students in dashboard-only arm
  • No increase among GutGPT-arm students
  • No increase among physicians
4.13.2 Explainability Perception
  • Students in dashboard-only arm: ↑ explainability perception
  • Physicians in GutGPT arm: ↑ explainability perception
4.14 Interpretation of Results
  • Students preferred ML dashboard because:
  • Specific risk scores (0–1) more translatable to clinical decisions
  • GutGPT caused cognitive overload and information uncertainty
  • Students struggled to synthesize GutGPT responses into decisions
  • Lower knowledge/experience made prompt formulation difficult
  • Physicians showed ↑ explainability perception in GutGPT arm because:
  • Higher experience and knowledge enabled verification of LLM responses
  • One senior resident: "GutGPT gave pretty accurate answers, consistent with clinical judgment"
  • LLM confirmed their initial decisions
4.15 Key Findings Summary
  • AI CDSS (with LLM) enhances knowledge and confidence, especially for medical students
  • Positive implications for medical education
  • Adoption propensity varies by arm and experience level
  • LLM may cause cognitive overload in less experienced users
4.16 Future Research Considerations
  • Durability of knowledge gains (follow-up at 3–6 months)
  • Subgroup analysis by physician seniority level
  • Integration into EMR systems (currently standalone)
5. Precision Medical Education Framework
5.1 Defining the Problem: Variable Fellow Needs
  • Juana (PGY-1): Strong technical skills, weak clinical reasoning → ahead endoscopically, behind clinically
  • Micah (PGY-1): Strong clinical reasoning, weak technical skills → ahead clinically, behind endoscopically
  • Both frustrated despite same curriculum and timeline
5.2 Evolution of GI Training Paradigms
  • Time-based training (pre-~2009): Time + experience = competence
  • Field expanded from 2 to 3 years due to complexity
  • Competency-based medical education (CBME): ~2012–present
  • Shift toward individualized competency progression
  • Precision medical education (PME): Current interstitial phase
  • Right educational intervention for right learner at right time
5.3 Challenges of CBME (Three Fundamental Problems)
5.3.1 Conceptual Complexity
  • Defining competence is simple in theory, complex in practice
  • Hard to observe subtle forms of performance (e.g., PDLI variations)
  • Milestone products sometimes hard to apply practically
5.3.2 Psychometric/Measurement Problems
  • Competence is not binary (not a single moment of achievement)
  • Learning curves vary wildly
  • Hard to judge individual competencies in real-time supervision
5.3.3 Logistical Problems
  • Attending-fellow interactions are intermittent
  • Unsupervised training impractical due to Medicare/Medicaid constraints
  • Huge administrative burden
5.4 PME Framework (AAMC Model)
  • Central to PME: data about the learner
  • Not snapshots → longitudinal data
  • Clinical experiences, competence measures, career goals
  • Analytics: AI transforms raw data into actionable insights
  • Interventions developed based on those insights
5.5 Four P's of PME
  • Proactive: Anticipating needs before problems manifest
  • Personalized: Individual-focused interventions
  • Participatory: Learner engaged in process
  • Predictive: AI-enabled pattern recognition for future risk
5.6 Funding and Current Research
  • AMA grants: $12,000,000 total for 4-year projects
  • Focus areas:
  • Individual and team-based clinical reasoning
  • Communication skills
  • Procedural skill certification
  • Coaching effectiveness
5.7 Operationalizing PME with AI
  • Inputs (data to collect):
  • Procedural data (colonoscopy skills assessments)
  • Clinical data
  • Knowledge measures
  • Behavioral data
  • AI-supported analysis:
  • Pattern recognition across all data points
  • Identification of skill gaps and trajectories
  • Output: Targeted insights for individual fellows
5.8 AI's Role in Learner Performance Analysis
  • Aggregates data systematically
  • Detects patterns humans might miss
  • Compares fellows to standards or peers
  • Identifies "learner phenotypes" (e.g., good technician vs. poor inspector)
5.9 Example: Colonoscopy Competency Breakdown
  • Technical skill (scope control, polyp removal) vs. cognitive skill (mucosal inspection)
  • AI can identify when fellow excels at one but not the other
  • Enables specific coaching conversations
5.10 Program-Wide Implementation Model

1. Curriculum + complementary measurements

2. CCC (Clinical Competency Committee) for outcomes assessment

3. AI-supported insights informing decisions

4. Coaching and curriculum adjustments

5. Program Evaluation Committee interventions

5.11 Immediate Actions for Implementing PME
  • Identify priority use case (colonoscopy, endoscopy, clinic, QI)
  • Assess what data is currently collected
  • Determine feasibility of new data collection
  • Ensure institutional secure AI system access
  • Engage stakeholders (faculty, fellows, staff)
  • Focus on learning (not just accreditation metrics)
5.12 Future Opportunities
  • Milestone improvements and practical EPA development
  • Innovative curricula and remediation strategies
  • Multi-institutional collaboration and dissemination
5.13 Key Principle: Human-AI Partnership
  • AI informs decisions but humans make decisions
  • AI transforms data into insights, humans provide context and judgment
  • Coaching and mentorship remain human responsibilities
6. Q&A Key Insights
6.1 CADDY Study Questions
  • Retrospective design may have affected results (variable attending usage patterns)
  • Future studies should track specific endoscopists longitudinally
  • Fellowship procedures excluded if attending had to intervene or remove polyps
  • Future analysis: stratify by years of attending experience (0–5, 5–10, etc.)
6.2 POEM AI Questions
  • Complexity classification (straightforward vs. complex) done manually by PI based on:
  • Esophageal anatomy
  • Procedure duration
  • Practical applications: training assessment and quality improvement
  • Future consideration: splitting tunneling into sub-phases (pre-LES vs. high-pressure zone)
  • Initial use likely retrospective, similar to ADR review workflow
6.3 Self-Assessment Tool Questions
  • Platform: Qstream (iterative neural network)
  • Questions dynamically generated from unique banks per stream
  • Experts writing questions in real-time based on guidelines (not solely AI-generated)
  • Partnership with ASGE for multi-center access
  • Algorithm focuses on topic more than specific questions
  • Fellows exposed to same question multiple times within topic
6.4 LLM Study Questions
  • Participants briefed on both dashboard and GutGPT before simulation
  • Could use GutGPT whenever they wanted (not mandated)
  • GutGPT trained only on UGI bleeding guidelines
  • Future: durability of knowledge gains at 3–6 months follow-up
  • GutGPT provides references from guidelines in responses
6.5 Precision Education Questions
6.5.1 Mentor-Learner Interaction with AI
  • Traditional apprenticeship learning (see one, do one, teach one) is valuable but unstructured
  • AI can complement by providing systematic data on learner competence
  • Still need human mentorship for synthesis and judgment
6.5.2 Learning to Work with AI
  • AI is becoming a competence for physicians (tool, product, subject)
  • Learners need to develop ability to interact with and provide feedback to AI systems
  • Co-piloting skills increasingly important
6.5.3 Collecting Continuous Quality Data
  • Workplace-based assessments are challenging for faculty to complete
  • Straight-lining of assessment forms common
  • Solutions:
  • Timely, focused assessments (e.g., procedure-specific skills forms)
  • Voice-recorded verbal summaries at end of day
  • Brief QR-code-based feedback systems
6.5.4 Real-Time AI During Procedures
  • Shared understanding of what feedback is needed
  • Pick 1–2 competencies per session to evaluate
  • Develop intention for the day/session
  • Strategic instruments (e.g., QR codes) to make feedback efficient
6.5.5 Learner-Driven Precision Education
  • Fellows want data on their own performance
  • Dashboards comparing to standards or peers can support self-reflection
  • Self-assessment must be tied to external feedback (not just reflection alone)
  • Coaching conversations can use dashboard data for development planning
7. Summary: Key Takeaways Across Presentations
7.1 AI in Adenoma Detection
  • CADe improves ADR/SDR across all experience levels
  • Greatest gains: first-year fellows (+10.3%) and attendings (~+15%)
  • Supports integration into fellowship training centers
7.2 AI in POEM Phase Recognition
  • Deep learning model achieves 94.2% accuracy across 87,915 test frames
  • Strongest performance in clinically critical phases (myotomy, tunneling, closure)
  • Future applications: training assessment and complication prevention
7.3 AI Adaptive Self-Assessment
  • Spaced repetition with iterative neural network improves proficiency across all levels
  • Targets weak areas (lowest baseline → highest gains)
  • Expansion to generative AI for real-time content creation underway
7.4 LLMs in Clinical Decision Support
  • LLM-enhanced CDSS improves knowledge and confidence (especially students)
  • Experienced physicians better able to verify LLM accuracy
  • Less experienced users may experience cognitive overload with LLMs
7.5 Precision Medical Education
  • PME: right intervention for right learner at right time
  • Requires continuous, granular data across competencies
  • AI enables pattern recognition and actionable insights from longitudinal data
  • Human judgment remains essential for decisions
7.6 Common Themes
  • AI supports but does not replace human judgment and mentorship
  • Data-driven approaches enable individualized training support
  • Need for longitudinal, systematic data collection
  • Integration of AI into training requires careful implementation and stakeholder engagement
