1. Acute Pancreatitis: 30-Day Readmission Prediction Model
1.1 Clinical Problem & Background
  • Acute pancreatitis --> 3rd most common GI hospitalization cause in the US
  • 22–25% of patients return within 30 days (ER or inpatient)
  • Many readmissions --> preventable
  • Existing scores (BISAP, Ransom's criteria) --> stratify in-hospital stay only
  • No validated discharge-focused score for this population
1.2 Study Objective
  • Primary: Build ML model using routine discharge data to predict 30-day readmission
  • Secondary: Distill model into simple bedside score for clinicians without computers
1.3 Cohort & Data Source
  • Database: MIMIC-IV version 3 (Beth Israel, critical care + inpatient data)
  • Full database: 546,000 hospitalizations from 2011–2021
  • Acute pancreatitis identified via:
  • ICD codes
  • Confirmed by: lipase >3× ULN OR imaging
  • Initial AP encounters: 1,916
  • Exclusions:
  • Pediatric patients
  • Multiple hospitalizations per patient (first only)
  • Patients who died in hospital or discharged to hospice
  • Final cohort: 4,362 adults
  • Primary outcome: 30-day unplanned readmission (ED revisit or inpatient)
  • Outcome prevalence in final cohort: 22.8%
1.4 Data Split
  • Training : Validation : Test = 60:20:20
  • Outcome prevalence preserved in each set
1.5 Predictor Domains (Available at/Before Discharge)
  • Demographics & etiology
  • Chronic disease burden (comorbidity)
  • Labs drawn within 24 hours of discharge:
  • CRP, albumin, hemoglobin, creatinine, bilirubin, glucose, triglycerides, calcium
  • Hospital course:
  • Length of stay (LOS)
  • ICU requirement
  • Pancreatic necrosis presence
  • Pancreatic fluid collection
  • Discharge disposition
  • Opioid exposure in final 48 hours of admission
  • Prior hospitalization within 6 months
1.6 Models Compared
Model Description
XGBoost (primary) Tree-based gradient boosting; captures nonlinear relationships & interaction effects
Multivariable logistic regression Traditional statistical benchmark with same predictors
LOS + BISAP curve Clinical familiar competitor to test if clinical intuition captures readmission risk
1.7 Evaluation Metrics
  • AUROC (Area Under ROC): measures discrimination (1 = perfect, 0.5 = chance)
  • AUPRC (Area Under Precision-Recall Curve): important for imbalanced outcomes (22.8% event rate)
  • Decision curve analysis: net benefit across risk thresholds vs treating all/none
  • Calibration score: ideal = 1.0 (predicted risk matches observed event rate)
1.8 Results: Model Performance
1.8.1 AUROC on Held-Out Test Set
  • XGBoost: 0.82 (95% CI: 0.80–0.84)
  • Simplified bedside score: 0.78
  • Logistic regression: 0.71
  • LOS + BISAP: 0.66
1.8.2 AUPRC (More Meaningful for Imbalanced Data)
  • Baseline event rate: 22.8% (red line in figures)
  • XGBoost: 0.46 (↑ doubled baseline)
  • Logistic regression: 0.34
  • Clinical competitors: ~0.30
1.8.3 Calibration
  • XGBoost calibration score: 0.98 (essentially perfect)
  • Example: When model predicts 35% risk → true observed rate ≈ 35%
1.9 SHAP Analysis: Top Predictors
1.9.1 Variable Importance (Mean |SHAP Value|)
  • #1: Opioid use in last 48 hours → SHAP value = 0.19
  • Patients with opioid use near discharge have unresolved pain
  • Pain --> primary driver for ED return
  • #2: CRP → SHAP value = 0.16
  • Marker of persistent systemic inflammation
  • #3: Albumin → SHAP value = 0.13
  • Reflects nutritional reserve and frailty
  • #4: Length of stay (short = paradoxically ↑ risk)
  • Likely reflects premature discharge or undertreated patients
  • #5: Peripancreatic fluid collection
  • #6: Prior hospitalization within 6 months
1.9.2 Direction of Effects
  • All six top predictors --> positive (↑ risk with higher values)
  • Short LOS paradox: early discharge may indicate premature discharge
1.10 Decision Curve Analysis
  • XGBoost (blue line) --> above all comparators across 10%–50% threshold range
  • Any reasonable intervention threshold using XGBoost --> identifies more true positives than alternatives
1.11 Simplified Bedside Score
  • Derived from top 6 predictors
  • Achieved AUROC 0.78
  • Clinicians can calculate without computer
1.12 Key Clinical Insights
  • ML captured complex interactions:
  • Opioid use weight differs based on inflammatory status, nutritional reserve
  • In-hospital BISAP not correlated with post-discharge vulnerability
  • All 6 predictors are actionable:
  • Opioid use → pain management review
  • Elevated CRP → incomplete resolution, needs early follow-up
  • Low albumin → nutrition support
1.13 Limitations
  • Single-center data (academic medical center)
  • Retrospective design → some planned readmissions may be included
  • External validation required
  • Generalizability to community hospitals uncertain
1.14 Discussion Points (Q&A)
  • Severity stratification considered but data limitations on Atlanta criteria classification
  • Primary outcome: all-cause vs pancreatic-specific → dataset limitation (no clear reason for ER visit)
  • CRP often not checked within 24h of discharge in routine practice
2. Pancreatic Cyst AI: IPMN Grading on EUS (Multicenter Study)
2.1 Background & Clinical Need
  • Pancreatic cyst prevalence: 13–18% of population
  • Prevalence ↑ with age
  • IPMN (Intraductal Papillary Mucinous Neoplasm) --> most common neoplastic lesion
  • ~80% of incidental discoveries are IPMNs
  • Current guidelines focus on high-grade dysplasia/invasive carcinoma identification
  • Limitations:
  • Limited FNA sensitivity
  • Complex clinical workflow (resource-intensive)
  • Heavy imaging resource use
2.2 AI Challenges in EUS
  • Complex data analysis required
  • Low accessibility (ground truth = pathology)
  • Most centers lack sufficient cyst cases for training
  • Need for tools that integrate into clinical workflow
  • Must work across different scopes, genders, ethnicities
2.3 Study Objective
  • Detect AND characterize IPMN grade (high vs low)
  • Novel approach: YOLO-based model ("You Only Look Once")
  • Both detection AND characterization capability
  • Previous work: detect pancreatic cysts, solid lesions, differentiate PDAC vs NET vs mucinous vs non-mucinous
2.4 Study Design
  • 40 EUS procedures from expert endoscopists across 3 continents
  • >21,000 images used for model development
  • Split: 90% training / 10% validation
  • 5-fold cross-validation to avoid overfitting
  • Ground truth: conclusive pathology with agreement between 2 expert pathologists
2.5 Model Performance Results
  • Interoperability: Works across different EUS processor brands
  • No major difference between male/female image analysis
  • Mean patient age: 67 years
2.5.1 Characterization (High vs Low Grade)
  • Overall accuracy: 89.1%
  • C-statistic (AUROC): 0.908 (>0.9 goal achieved)
2.5.2 Detection
  • C-statistic: 0.917 (>0.9 goal achieved)
2.6 Key Innovation
  • Detection + characterization combined (vs previous models that did one or the other)
  • Interoperability feature: patented technology
  • Adapts layers to different image sizes across brands
  • Essential for multi-institutional, multi-ethnic deployment
2.7 Future Directions
  • Fully comprehensive pancreatic AI system:
  • Integrate cystic + solid lesion models
  • Local/regional staging (node identification)
  • Goal: Multi-model system answering all relevant clinical questions
2.8 Take-Home Messages
  • Need more accurate, objective risk stratification
  • Better identification of high-risk patients → timelier referral for surgery
  • Accurate identification of low-risk patients → avoid unnecessary pancreatic surgery morbidity
  • AI enables precision medicine: surveillance vs intervention decisions
2.9 Limitations & Considerations
  • Gold standard = surgery/histology or FNA (pathology-based)
  • Pathologists must agree on low vs high grade
  • Even pathology can fail → required 2-expert agreement for ground truth
  • Model quality depends on ground truth quality
2.10 Subtype Analysis (Future Work)
  • IPMN subtypes: intestinal, gastric, pancreaticobiliary
  • Subtype prediction may be clinically relevant (e.g., pancreaticobiliary → higher risk even years later)
  • Currently analyzing in larger cohort (300 patients)
3. AI Impact on Endosonographer Performance: Solid Pancreatic Tumors
3.1 Study Context
  • Early pancreatic cancer detection remains challenging
  • High EUS lesion miss rate despite high sensitivity (especially subtle lesions)
  • AI may improve detection by supporting real-time interpretation during dynamic EUS
3.2 Study Objective
  • Evaluate AI software (MyCompanion by iGlobe) on:
  • Lesion detection accuracy
  • Diagnostic confidence in EUS assessment of solid pancreatic tumors
3.3 Study Design
  • Randomized controlled tandem video reader study
  • Videos: 2-minute clips of actual EUS exams
  • All confirmed by histology (virgin clips not used in AI training/validation)
  • 3 sequential experiments with increasing detection difficulty
  • Mix of advanced and novice endosonographers
3.4 Dataset & Participants
  • 38 curated pancreatitis EUS video clips
  • Includes true positive and true negative cases
  • All histology-confirmed
  • 57 endosonographers with varying experience levels
3.5 Randomization & Method
  • First reading: with AI vs without AI (computer-generated randomization)
  • AI output: real-time lesion identification via bounding boxes
  • Single-pass video review
  • Freeze frame with grid for marking lesions
  • Confidence rated on 3-point Likert scale
3.6 Results: Overall Detection Accuracy
3.6.1 All Participants Combined
  • Without AI: 70.8%
  • With AI: 77.8% (7% absolute ↑)
3.6.2 By Experiment Difficulty
Experiment Lesion Characteristics Without AI With AI Absolute Improvement
1 (Easy) Straightforward lesions Baseline ~Same None
2 (Medium) Moderate difficulty Lower ↑ Significant
3 (Hard) Subtle lesions, <2cm, difficult parenchyma Lowest 11.5% ↑ Most pronounced
3.7 Results: By Experience Level
  • Novice and experienced both benefited in experiments 2–3
  • No significant benefit in experiment 1 (easy cases)
  • Unexpected finding: even expert endosonographers showed improvement
3.8 Diagnostic Confidence
  • Significantly improved with AI assistance on Likert scale
3.9 Key Findings
  • AI assistance significantly improved tumor detection and diagnostic confidence
  • Benefit observed in both novice AND experienced endosonographers
  • Most pronounced benefit for subtle, small (<2cm) lesions in difficult cases
3.10 Limitations Acknowledged
  • Benchtop study (not real-time clinical workflow)
  • Real-world factors (scope handling, rescanning) not captured
  • Future: ongoing clinical studies needed
3.11 Discussion Points (Q&A)
  • Workflow integration: Most cases already detected by other imaging first
  • AI may be most useful in screening EUS (decrease miss rate)
  • Quality standardization: tool trained on both poor and good quality images
  • Multi-center, multi-device data used for training
4. Deep Learning for Pancreatic Cancer Detection on CT (Spark Model)
4.1 Clinical Problem
  • Early-stage pancreatic cancer often missed on CT
  • ~38% of stage IV patients had subtle CT features at prior imaging
  • Stage I survival: >83%
  • Late diagnosis (stage IV) --> 3rd most lethal cancer in US
  • Problem: not detection failure but recognition failure of subtle patterns
4.2 Study Objective
  • Use deep learning to recognize subtle CT features suggesting early pancreatic cancer
  • Create accessible tool (web-based application)
4.3 Dataset & Ground Truth
  • ~2,000 CT images obtained from multiple institutions worldwide
  • Radiologists from Yale and Augusta University classified images:
  • Benign vs malignant
  • Staged according to diagnosis
4.4 Model Architecture: EfficientNetB4
  • Deep learning model for image-based analysis
  • ~6–8 million parameters (chosen for balance of accuracy vs computational efficiency)
  • Alternative models use 100–150 million parameters
  • Reason for choice: avoid overfitting, maintain reproducibility
4.5 Data Split & Training
  • 3:1 ratio for training criteria
  • 60% of images → training set (model learns patterns)
  • Remaining 40%:
  • 20% → internal validation
  • 20% → external validation (reproducibility testing)
4.6 Model Training Process
  • CT image → segmented to pancreas region only
  • EfficientNetB4 processes through convolutional neural network layers
  • Augmentation: AI manipulates images in different angles/dimensions to learn patterns (not just memorize)
  • Goal: model learns process, not just recognition
4.7 Deployment Strategy
  • Model hosted on website for universal access
  • Does NOT require high-performance computing (GPU)
  • Accessible on standard computers globally
4.8 Performance Metrics
4.8.1 Internal Validation
  • Accuracy: >99%
  • AUROC, F1 score, F2 score all high
4.8.2 External Validation (via website)
  • Expected dip due to: data loss, internet issues, platform efficiency
  • Still maintained high accuracy
4.8.3 Confusion Matrix Results (External Validation)
  • Near-perfect classification
  • Minimal false positives and negatives
4.9 Computational Efficiency
Task Time
Single image diagnosis 0.7 seconds
1,000 images <60 seconds
4.10 Clinical Application
  • Goal: augment radiologist, not replace
  • Earlier diagnosis → improved survival
  • Improves equity of access (resource-limited settings)
  • Decreases time to diagnosis
4.11 Limitations Acknowledged
  • Training dataset: only 400 CT images (small)
  • Currently recruiting more data for better training
  • Future: multi-institutional study with diverse CT images
  • Still investigating exact parameters model uses for detection
4.12 Future Directions
  • Multi-institutional collaboration planned
  • Expand dataset to improve model robustness
  • Identify which subtle features AI recognizes (explainability)
  • Goal: help humans diagnose without AI in future
5. Multi-Organ Radiomics for Early PDAC Prediction (6–24 Months Before Diagnosis)
5.1 Background & Rationale
  • No current screening program for general population (PDAC incidence: ~13/100,000)
  • High-risk subgroup annual incidence: ~1%
  • AI-driven CT analysis could identify high-risk individuals from average-risk pool
  • Prior study (Mayo Clinic 2022):
  • ML on CTs acquired 3–36 months before diagnosis
  • Model AUC: 0.98 vs human readers at 0.66
  • Limitation: ~40% had pancreatic abnormalities on radiologist review
5.2 Study Objective
  • Create imaging biomarker integrating:
  • Multi-organ radiomics features
  • Body composition features from clinical CT scans
  • Goal: identify risk-enriched cohort for pancreatic cancer
5.3 Study Design
  • Retrospective case-control study
  • Cases: pathologically confirmed PDAC with incidental CTs acquired 2–60 months before diagnosis
  • Controls: matched 3:1 to cases on:
  • Race, sex, year of CT, IV contrast status
  • Exclusion: patients with cancer 60 months prior to and within 6 months after CT
5.4 Feature Extraction Methods
  • Deep learning automated segmentation for organ identification
  • Extracted features:
  • ~75 radiomics features per organ (pancreas, liver, spleen, kidney)
  • Body composition: cross-sectional area + attenuation from L3 vertebrae
  • Skeletal muscle
  • Adipose tissue (subcutaneous, visceral)
5.5 Rationale for Multi-Organ + Body Composition
  • Skeletal muscle wasting detectable up to 18 months before diagnosis
  • Adipose tissue changes detectable up to 6 months before diagnosis
  • These systemic changes precede clinical symptoms
5.6 Model Development Pipeline

1. Batch harmonization: Remove scanner-related effects from radiomics values

2. Feature selection: Mann-Whitney test (p < 0.1) to keep features associated with case/control status

3. Regularization: Remove correlated features to prevent overfitting

4. Normalization of feature set

5. Input into 5 different ML classifiers → evaluated on test set

5.7 Data Split
  • Training/validation: 80%
  • Test set: 20% (used for final evaluation)

5.9 Risk Score Progression
  • Significant ↑ trend in risk scores as patients approach diagnosis
  • Significant differences from controls across ALL pre-diagnostic windows
  • Shows subtle changes detectable even 1–3 years before clinical diagnosis
5.10 Feature Importance Analysis
  • 2–5 months pre-diagnosis: Pancreas shape features dominant
  • Top feature: pancreas spherical disproportion
  • Reflects morphological changes as cancer develops
  • 25–60 months pre-diagnosis: Body composition + liver features dominant
  • Top features: subcutaneous fat attenuation, visceral adipose tissue
  • 6–24 months pre-diagnosis: Mixed features from all domains
  • Pancreas shape/texture + liver diameter/cluster shade + adipose tissues
5.11 Model Performance by Tumor Characteristics
  • No significant difference based on:
  • Metastatic status
  • Tumor site of origin
  • Conclusion: radiomics signature reflects systemic changes, not just local tumor
5.12 Key Clinical Implications
  • Multi-organ approach captures sparse, subtle signals across different organs
  • Even when pancreas appears normal → systemic radiomic changes detectable
  • Model may identify candidates for intensified screening 6–24 months before diagnosis
5.13 Limitations
  • Retrospective design
  • CT indications varied (mostly pain-related)
  • Limited data on new-onset diabetes impact
  • External validation needed
5.14 Future Directions
  • Apply model in low-prevalence populations (more representative of general population)
  • Integrate with other screening tools:
  • EMR-based risk models
  • Blood/fluid biomarkers
  • Create multimodal screening tool for high-risk identification
5.15 Discussion Points (Q&A)
  • Comparison to prior body composition model:
  • Body composition alone: AUC 0.57
  • Multi-organ + body composition: AUC 0.72 (significant improvement)
  • Diabetes consideration:
  • Cases more likely to have new-onset diabetes vs controls
  • Future analysis planned with larger cohort
6. Cross-Cutting Themes & Synthesis
6.1 Common Clinical Applications of AI in Pancreatic Disease
  • Early detection (subtle features humans miss)
  • Risk stratification (readmission, malignancy risk)
  • Decision support (bedside scores, clinical integration)
6.2 Key Technical Considerations
  • Ground truth quality is paramount (pathology confirmation preferred)
  • Multi-institutional data needed for generalizability
  • Interoperability across devices/brands essential for real-world deployment
  • Explainability important for clinical adoption (SHAP analysis, feature importance)
6.3 Data Requirements & Limitations
  • Single-center → retrospective → limited generalizability
  • Need diverse datasets (ethnicities, institutions, scanner types)
  • Small sample sizes in some studies → need validation
6.4 Clinical Workflow Integration
  • Web-based applications for accessibility
  • EHR integration potential (automated alerts)
  • Augmentation, not replacement of clinical judgment
6.5 Regulatory & Validation Considerations
  • External validation required before clinical deployment
  • Prospective studies needed for real-world confirmation
  • Commercial products emerging (e.g., MyCompanion in Europe)
6.6 Future Directions Across All Studies
  • Multi-modal models (imaging + clinical + biomarkers)
  • Larger, prospective cohorts
  • Real-time deployment studies
  • Patient-centered outcomes (mortality reduction, quality of life)
