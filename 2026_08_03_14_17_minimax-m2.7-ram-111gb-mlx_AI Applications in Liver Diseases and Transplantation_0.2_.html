1. AI in Donor Liver Discard Decisions
1.1 Clinical Problem
  • 12,000+ donor livers added to waitlist annually in US
  • Only 61% undergo transplantation within 3 years
  • 3 people die on waitlist daily due to organ shortage
  • ~10% of recovered donor livers are not utilized
  • Gap exists between guidelines and real-world discard decisions
1.2 Key Factors for Donor Liver Discard
  • Donor characteristics: age, BMI, serum ALT levels
  • Organ factors: macrovascular steatosis percentage on biopsy
  • Procedure factors: cold ischemia time, arginine administration
  • Guidelines provide recommended thresholds for these parameters
1.3 Decision Tree Model Analysis
  • Dataset: UNOS (multicenter, 4 decades of US transplant practice)
  • ~10% of donor livers in dataset were discarded
  • Model identified top decision factors:
1.3.1 Single Most Important Factor
  • Macrovascular steatosis identified as #1 factor
  • Donor liver with fat content >19.5% → high discard likelihood
1.3.2 Two Most Important Factors
  • Macrovascular steatosis remains most important
  • For fat content <19.5%: age emerges as key factor
  • Donor age <35.5 years → higher transplant possibility
1.3.3 Three Most Important Factors
  • Macrovascular steatosis → #1 factor
  • For fat content <19.5% + donor age <35.5 years: serum ALT becomes important
  • ALT <39.75 → high chance of transplant
  • For fat content <19.5% + donor age >35.5 years: arginine amount matters
  • Donor received >0.796 arginine before harvest → higher transplant chance
1.4 Model Accuracy
  • One-depth decision tree: ~75% accuracy (macrosteatosis as single factor)
  • Auto-depth decision tree: achieves similar high accuracy
  • Cutoffs learned from data vs. guideline thresholds differ significantly
1.5 Key Findings: Guidelines vs. Real-World Practice
  • Cutoff values learned from data are more conservative than published guidelines
  • Transplant centers apply more conservative thresholds for:
  • Steatosis in donor liver organs
  • Donor age limits
  • This causes unnecessary discard of otherwise usable donor organs
  • Represents missed opportunity to reduce waitlist mortality
1.6 Future Directions
  • Time period level analysis (compare early 2000s vs. 1990s vs. current practice)
  • Center-level/geographic analysis of transplant practices
  • Identify systemic gaps between guidelines and actual discard behavior
2. AI in Predicting HCC Treatment
2.1 Clinical Problem
  • Treatment allocation for HCC traditionally discussed in multidisciplinary tumor boards
  • Current decision-making = sum of oncologic knowledge + physician experience
  • Classical statistics provide population-level predictions (e.g., 80% survive, 20% die)
  • Individual patient prognosis remains uncertain in clinical practice
2.2 Model Development
  • Dataset: ~16,000 patients from two large Italian registries
  • Training/validation split: 80%/20%
  • Bootstrap internal validation performed
  • External validation cohort from University of Tokyo
  • Final model: ~7,000 patients across all treatment types
2.2.1 Treatment Distribution in Dataset
  • Liver resection: ~4,000 patients
  • Primary liver transplantation: ~100 patients
  • Thermoablation: ~1,487 patients
  • Chemoembolization: ~1,164 patients
  • Systemic therapies: ~181 patients
2.2.2 Model Architecture
  • LASSO regression for variable selection
  • Random Forest model with 1,000 bootstrap validations
  • Target: AUC optimization
2.3 Model Performance
  • Internal validation:
  • 1-year AUC: 83%
  • 5-year AUC: 76%
  • External validation (Japanese cohort):
  • 5-year AUC: 72%
2.4 Treatment Allocation Analysis
  • Model predicts survival under each treatment for individual patients
  • Treatment with highest predicted survival = recommended option
2.4.1 Reallocation Findings
  • Liver transplantation:
  • Delivered in reality: only 1.5% of cases
  • Best choice at 5 years: up to 22% of cases
  • Embolization:
  • Delivered in reality: 16% of patients
  • Best strategy at 5 years: only 1.7% of cases
2.4.2 Time Point Analysis (Alluvial Plot)
  • At 1 year: 27% of patients completely reallocated by model
  • At 5 years: 55% of patients completely reallocated by model
  • Model can identify optimal treatment strategy at different time horizons
2.5 Survival Gain Analysis
  • Median predicted gain:
  • At 1 year: +2% survival benefit
  • At 5 years: +24% survival benefit (if model recommendations followed)
2.6 Clinical Application: Augmented Multidisciplinary Meeting
  • Web-based application for clinical use
  • Input: all patient variables from tumor board discussion
  • Output: survival predictions under each treatment option
  • Model explains why specific treatment recommended for that patient
2.7 Why Random Forest Was Chosen
  • Tested multiple models: XGBoost, SVM, neural networks, random forest
  • Random Forest provided best metrics after testing
  • Best performance-to-complexity ratio among alternatives
2.8 Limitations and Considerations
  • Model provides oncologic knowledge only (not technical feasibility)
  • Local expertise determines if recommended treatment is available
  • Model serves as simulator → final decision remains with clinical team
3. AI-Based Screening for Advanced Fibrosis
3.1 Clinical Problem: MASH/NASH Burden
  • ~1/3 of all adults globally affected by MASLD
  • US: 50 million adults have MASH; ~7 million already at F2+ fibrosis
  • MASH is leading driver of cirrhosis, HCC, and death
  • Estimated $76 billion in direct healthcare costs (US)
  • Majority of advanced fibrosis remains undiagnosed
  • Current screening pathways have two major limitations:
  • Imperfect sensitivity (miss true advanced fibrosis)
  • Low real-world completion rates
3.2 Current Guidelines Limitations
  • Sequential approach: FIB4 → elastography
  • Patients often never screened or fall through cracks
  • Results in delayed diagnosis, delayed treatment, worse outcomes
3.3 AI Model Used: FibroX (LGBoost)
  • Explainable LGBoost model developed at Yale
  • Includes >20 clinical inputs from EHR records
  • Automatically screens and flags high-risk patients
  • Can explain why patient is being flagged (clinician-facing)
  • AUC: 97% vs. FIB4 at 62%
  • Largest performance gap reported in MASLD literature
3.4 Study Methodology
  • Markov state-transition model simulating 13 health states:
  • Fibrosis stages → cirrhosis → HCC → transplant → death
  • Patients enter at MASLD/early fibrosis stage
  • Transitions occur annually
3.4.1 Strategies Compared (4 total)

1. AI followed by elastography

2. FIB4 followed by elastography

3. Elastography alone

4. No screening

3.4.2 Treatment Scenarios Modeled
  • Semaglutide (GLP-1 agonist)
  • Resmetirom (THR-β agonist) - both FDA-approved for NASH
3.4.3 Economic Outcomes Analyzed
  • Total cost
  • QALYs (Quality-Adjusted Life Years)
  • Combines length and quality of life
  • 1 year in perfect health = 1 QALY
  • ICER (Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratio)
  • Lower is better for ICER
  • How much extra cost per additional QALY gained
  • Net Monetary Benefit (higher = better)
  • Willingness-to-pay threshold: $50,000-$100,000 in US
3.5 Results: Base Case Analysis (10-year horizon)
  • AI + elastography provided:
  • Highest QALYs
  • Lowest ICER (below $100,000 threshold)
  • Both semaglutide and resmetirom showed ICER lower than $100,000 threshold
  • Other strategies had significantly higher ICERs
3.6 Cost-Effectiveness Curves
  • AI + elastography consistently highest cost-effective strategy
  • Remained optimal even at lower willingness-to-pay thresholds
  • Results robust under uncertainty
3.7 Direct Comparison: Resmetirom vs. Semaglutide
  • Semaglutide dominates resmetirom
  • Higher probability of being most effective
  • Lower willingness-to-pay threshold needed
  • Shows value of screening is linked to treatment economics
3.8 Why AI + Elastography Outperforms
  • Better identification of high-risk patients
  • Earlier initiation and treatment → reduces progression to complications
  • Long-term benefit offsets upfront costs despite higher initial screening cost
3.9 Real-World Implications
  • AI-enabled training can integrate directly into EHR systems
  • Enables scalable identification of high-risk patients
  • Represents high-value population health strategy for payers/health systems
3.10 Study Limitations
  • Model-based analysis dependent on published literature inputs
  • Clinical trial data used for treatment effects (real-world adherence may differ)
  • AI model parameterized using published performance metrics
  • Analysis from payer perspective only (not societal)
  • Real-world implementation costs not fully captured
4. AI Chatbot for Hepatic Encephalopathy Detection
4.1 Clinical Background
  • Hepatic encephalopathy (HE): neurocognitive complication of cirrhosis
  • Range: sleep-wake disturbance → disorientation → confusion → coma
  • Estimated >40% of cirrhosis patients over 5 years
  • Covert/Minimal HE:
  • Lacks clinical symptoms detectable on routine exam
  • Deficits present on validated scoring tools (e.g., PHES)
  • Prevalence: ~41% globally in cirrhosis patients
  • Up to 60% prevalence in Child C cirrhosis
  • Diagnosis is time-consuming, requires dedicated testing
4.2 Clinical Gap
  • ~40% of AASLD providers have never performed dedicated CHE testing
  • Associated with progression to overt HE and poor quality of life
4.3 Study Objective
  • Develop text-based chatbot for covert hepatic encephalopathy (CHE) detection
  • Deployable at scale through patient portals (e.g., Epic MyChart)
  • Goal: reduce friction in CHE diagnosis
4.4 Study Design
  • Pilot study: 20 patients with chronic liver disease
  • Cases: 10 (PHES positive for CHE)
  • Controls: 10 (PHES negative)
  • Outpatient clinic visits
4.4.1 Exclusion Criteria
  • History of overt HE
  • Ongoing psychotropic medications (including opiates)
  • Non-English fluent participants
4.5 Chatbot Platform: Laversa
  • Large language model (UCSF-based, OpenAI architecture)
  • Uses Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG)
  • Ground truth embedding without parameter hyper-tuning
  • Trained on ~30 society guideline documents
  • HIPAA and PHI compliant
  • Designed for 6th-grade reading level, short answers
4.6 Chat Session Protocol
  • Supervised by clinical research coordinator (CRC)
  • CRC present to troubleshoot technical issues only
  • No specific instructions given on:
  • What topics to discuss
  • Session length
  • Mimicked natural patient-portal interaction
4.7 Data Processing Pipeline

1. Text transcripts exported from chat sessions

2. Input to text embedding model (OpenAI)

3. Converts text → high-dimensional vectors (>3,000 dimensions)

4. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction

5. Result: 16 components explaining ~95% of variance

4.8 Models Tested (4 total)
  • K-means clustering (unsupervised)
  • Decision tree
  • Convolutional neural network
  • Logistic regression
4.9 Cohort Characteristics
  • Median age: 67 years
  • >50% male participants
  • Case group median MELD: 9.5
  • Etiology distribution:
  • MASLD/MASH: 7 patients
  • Alcohol-related liver disease: next most common
  • Viral hepatitis, autoimmune, PBC, polycystic, hemochromatosis: others
4.10 Model Performance
  • K-means clustering showed best performance among models tested
  • Test characteristics: ~0.60 (better than chance)
  • Other supervised models showed wide variance
  • Poor performance in leave-one-out cross-validation (small sample size)
  • Likely due to high dimensionality despite PCA
4.11 Exploratory Analysis: Change Detection
  • 6 patients from case group returned for second visit
  • Second chat transcripts converted to embedding space
  • PCA on change between visits: 5 components explaining ~95% of variance
  • Spearman correlations performed with final PHES score
4.11.1 Key Finding
  • Principal Component 3 showed significant negative correlation with final PHES score
  • Suggests embedding space variation may correlate with CHE severity
4.12 Study Limitations
  • Selection bias: participants were in-person clinic visitors (older, less tech-savvy)
  • Some patients had difficulty engaging/typing with chatbot
  • Wide variance in transcript length and content richness
  • CRC presence likely influenced patient behavior (felt observed)
  • Target population (chronic liver disease) may struggle with technology if encephalopathic
4.13 Future Directions
  • Validation in larger datasets and real-world contexts
  • Deployment through patient portals (MyChart) analyzing existing messages
  • Automated flagging of clinicians based on patient message content over time
5. Explainable AI for Cirrhosis Survival Prediction (XGBoost)
5.1 Clinical Problem
  • Cirrhosis: ~1 million hospitalizations annually in US
  • 20% of admitted cirrhosis patients die within 90 days
  • Once decompensated: >50% dead within 1 year
  • Critical window for ICU escalation, transplant evaluation, or goals of care discussions is narrow
5.2 Limitations of Existing Scores
  • MELD: AUC ~0.78 (acceptable but not excellent)
  • Child-Pugh: Static snapshot, doesn't evolve with patient
  • CLIF-C ACLF: Primarily for ICU patients
  • All existing scores rely exclusively on lab values
  • None incorporate: MAP, lactate, vasopressor requirement, multi-organ dysfunction signals
5.3 Study Objectives

1. Validate XGBoost model built from rich EHR data (MIMIC-IV)

2. Benchmark performance against logistic regression and MELD

3. Use SHAP analysis to explain predictions

5.4 Data Source: MIMIC-IV
  • 14 years of data (2008-2022)
  • Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center
  • >400,000 unique patient features
  • >500,000 hospitalizations
  • IRB exempt (de-identified per HIPAA)
5.5 Cohort Selection
  • Total hospitalizations: ~364,627
  • ICD codes for cirrhosis/advanced liver failure → 16,XXX patients
  • Index admissions only (no readmissions)
  • Exclusions: prior transplant, active malignancy, missing labs, LOS <24h, age <18
  • Final cohort: ~11,000 patients
5.6 Feature Domains
  • Demographics: age, sex, race, insurance status
  • Comorbidities: ALD, NASH, viral hepatitis
  • Admission vitals
  • First 24-hour labs: BILI, Cr, INR, platelets, LFTs, lactate
  • Organ support: vasopressors, mechanical ventilation, renal replacement
5.7 Outcome Definition
  • Primary outcome: 90-day transplant-free survival
  • Absence of all-cause death or liver transplant within 90 days
5.8 Model Pipeline
  • XGBoost (gradient boosted tree ensemble)
  • Hyperparameter tuning: Bayesian optimization
  • 100 trials, 5-fold stratified cross-validation
  • Comparator: L2-regularized logistic regression (standardized features)
  • Benchmark: MELD score
5.9 Evaluation Metrics
  • AUROC (discrimination)
  • Brier score (lower = better, calibration)
  • Decision curve analysis
  • Sensitivity and specificity
5.10 Cohort Characteristics: Survivors vs. Non-Survivors
Feature Survivors Non-Survivors
Median age 58 years 62 years
MELD score 16 27
Total BILI 2.1 mg/dL 7.08 mg/dL (~4x higher)
Serum lactate 1.6 mmol/L 3.4 mmol/L (~2x higher)

Vasopressors (24h) 3.3% 23.9% (~7x higher)
Mechanical ventilation 2.1% 18%
5.11 Model Performance Results
  • XGBoost AUC: 0.89 (primary result)
  • MELD AUC: 0.78
  • Improvement: +14% discrimination over MELD
5.11.1 Decision Curve Analysis
  • XGBoost dominates all comparators across entire clinical range
  • Gap of 15-35% between XGBoost and MELD in clinically meaningful threshold zone
5.12 Key Predictors (SHAP Analysis)
  • Total bilirubin - strongest predictor
  • Sodium
  • INR
  • MAP (hemodynamic compromise)
  • Serum lactate (shock physiology)
  • Vasopressor use
  • SHAP values: 0.19-0.17 for top predictors
5.13 Performance Across Clinical Subgroups
  • Alcohol-related cirrhosis: AUC ~0.90
  • NASH and viral hepatitis subgroups: high performance maintained
  • MELD 8+ subgroup: AUC ~0.86
  • ICU vs. general ward admission: both subgroups perform well
5.14 Calibration Analysis
  • XGBoost: Brier score ~0.97 (excellent calibration)
  • MELD: Brier score ~0.80
  • XGBoost slightly outperformed on perfect calibration metric
5.15 Clinical Utility and Conclusions
  • XGBoost significantly outperforms MELD in discrimination
  • Incorporates dynamic vitals, lactate, organ perfusion signals
  • SHAP enables patient-level explanation of why score is high
  • Clinical applications:
  • Better triage within first 24 hours
  • Improved transplant referral timing
  • Enhanced discharge planning
5.16 Limitations
  • Single-center dataset (retrospective design)
  • No prospective implementation tested yet
  • MIMIC does not capture pre-admission outpatient trajectory
  • Transplant listing decisions not in dataset
  • Endpoint is surrogate marker
5.17 Next Steps
  • External validation across institutions
  • EMR integration with real-time alerts for high-risk patients
  • Red flag system within first 24 hours of admission
6. Cell-Free DNA for Non-Invasive Liver Disease Detection
6.1 Clinical Problem
  • Incidence/mortality from liver disease increasing over two decades
  • ~1 in 4 US adults have undiagnosed MASLD
  • Liver disease exists on continuum: steatosis → fibrosis → cirrhosis → HCC
  • Even among those with fibrosis, effective treatments exist
  • Among cirrhotics: lifetime HCC risk ~1 in 3
  • <20% of high-risk patients receive guideline-adherent liver cancer screening
6.2 Cell-Free DNA (cfDNA) Biology
  • DNA of nucleosomal origin, freely circulating after cell death
  • In cancer patients: small proportion directly from tumor
  • First-generation liquid biopsies looked for specific mutations → limited early-stage sensitivity
6.3 Novel Approach: Cell-Free DNA Fragmentome
  • Full compendium of tens of millions of cfDNA fragments from <1mL blood
  • Genome-wide fragment length/distribution reflects:
  • Genomic changes
  • Epigenomic changes
  • Chromatin state alterations in cancer
  • Assayed using inexpensive, low-coverage whole genome sequencing
6.4 Computational Approaches Developed
6.4.1 DELFI (DNA Evaluation of Fragments for Early Interception)
  • Analyzes genome-wide length distributions of cfDNA fragments
  • Healthy individuals: tightly regulated around 167 base pairs (nucleosome length)
  • Cancer: chaotic fragmentation profiles due to altered DNA packaging
  • Uses short-to-long fragment ratio across genome
6.4.2 ARTEMIS (Analysis of Repeat Elements in Disease)
  • Looks at "dark genome" (LINEs, SINEs, transposable elements)
  • Historically excluded due to alignment challenges
  • Alignment-free approach to reconstruct global repeat landscape
  • Uses machine learning on combined fragmentation + repeat data
6.5 Liver Cancer Detection Results
  • Cross-validation across chromosomal arms (5 Mb bins)
  • Healthy individuals: very consistent fragment ratios
  • Cancer patients: aberrant patterns throughout genome
  • Model validated in external cohort from separate institution
6.6 Extending to Non-Malignant Liver Disease
  • Signal present even in cirrhosis (pre-cancer)
  • Study assembled with discovery and validation cohorts:
  • Healthy livers
  • Early liver disease/fibrosis
  • Advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis
6.6.1 Fragmentome Changes in Liver Disease
  • Fragments overall slightly shorter in cirrhosis vs. healthy
  • Changes observed in:
  • Fragmentation profiles
  • Repeat landscape (transposable elements)
  • Distribution at epigenetic hotspots
6.7 Combined Classifier Performance
  • DELFI + ARTEMIS combined classifier trained on both features
  • External validation in separate institutional cohorts
6.7.1 Detection Rates (External Validation)
  • Early liver disease/fibrosis: ~50%
  • Advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis: >80%
6.8 Comparison with FIB4
  • FIB4: Age + platelets + AST/ALT (not designed for early fibrosis)
  • In study cohorts: FIB4 failed to detect early fibrosis
  • cfDNA fragmentome approach showed potential for earlier detection
6.9 Methylome Analysis (Mechanism Investigation)
  • Whole methylome sequencing on cfDNA from subset of individuals
  • Cell-type deconvolution revealed:
  • Changes in immune cell cfDNA contribution
  • Changes in liver-specific cell cfDNA contribution
6.9.1 Cell-of-Origin Analysis
  • Unmethylated fragments from altered cell types were shorter in cirrhosis
  • Unique methylation positions mapped to regions:
  • Overall enriched for short fragments in cirrhosis
  • Confirms liver-specific signal in cfDNA
6.10 Cross-Reactivity Testing
  • Evaluated model specificity in other fibrotic conditions:
  • Chronic pancreatitis
  • Benign lung nodules
  • Autoimmune/inflammatory processes
  • Low scores in these non-liver conditions
6.11 Cancer vs. Non-Cancer Signal Differentiation
  • Liver cancer model (y-axis): elevated in malignancy, NOT elevated in fibrosis/cirrhosis
  • Liver disease model (x-axis): elevated in both malignant AND non-malignant pathology
  • Likely reflects fibrotic origins of many liver cancers
6.12 Clinical Implementation Potential
  • cfDNA test can:
  • Screen for fibrotic liver disease (earlier detection)
  • Route patients to appropriate care pathways
  • Identify those needing liver cancer surveillance
  • Complementary to existing fragmentomic blood test for cancer detection
6.13 Future Directions
  • Translation in diverse clinical settings underway
  • Development for both cancer and non-malignant chronic conditions
  • Ongoing validation across populations and healthcare systems
7. Session Synthesis: Key Themes Across Studies
7.1 Common Methodological Approaches
  • Machine learning models outperform traditional clinical scores
  • Multi-feature integration captures complex relationships
  • Explainable AI (SHAP, LIME) essential for clinical trust
7.2 Economic and Implementation Considerations
  • AI screening can be cost-effective at population scale
  • Integration into EHR systems enables real-world deployment
  • Upfront costs offset by downstream prevention benefits
7.3 Clinical Translation Gaps
  • Single-center/retrospective data needs external validation
  • Real-world adherence may differ from clinical trial settings
  • Provider education and workflow integration required
7.4 Emerging Applications in Hepatology
  • Organ utilization: reducing unnecessary discard of donor livers
  • Treatment selection: personalized HCC therapy recommendations
  • Population screening: identifying MASLD/MASH patients needing intervention
  • Digital health: chatbot-based cognitive assessment tools
  • Liquid biopsy: cfDNA fragmentome for fibrosis/cirrhosis detection
