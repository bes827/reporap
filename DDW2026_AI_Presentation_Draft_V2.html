The Eight Questions Every Clinician Is Asking

1. "Is AI truly accurate in real-world settings?"

Source: AI Colonoscopy and Adenoma Detection | Lines: 193–214, 276–319

The evidence reveals a persistent gap between controlled trials and real-world practice:

Controlled RCTs show benefit:

  • Meta-analysis of 44 RCTs: ↑ adenomas per colonoscopy with CADe, ↑ ADR with CADe
  • No difference in advanced colorectal neoplasia per colonoscopy; only small increase in ACN detection rates

Pragmatic real-world trials tell a different story:

  • coloDETECT: Improved detection, but operators knew they were in a trial (Hawthorne effect)
  • **NAIAD **(UK): ↑ ADR with CADe, but returned to baseline when device was switched off
  • Australian study: ADR improved only when CADe was adopted; lowest-ADR endoscopists did not use it

**The definitive test — Barkin's triple-blinded RCT **(1,744 patients)

  • Fully concealed design: Patients unaware of trial participation; endoscopy staff unaware of trial existence; data analysts blinded to allocation
  • This removes the Hawthorne effect entirely — operators behave as they normally would
  • **ITT analysis **(CADe available in room): No significant ADR difference between groups
  • **Per-protocol analysis **(CADe actually used): >5% absolute ADR improvement — significant
  • Advanced lesion detection: Significantly improved in BOTH ITT and per-protocol analyses — the key finding
  • CADe utilization rate: Only 57.8% overall (Center 1: ~70%, Center 2: <40%, Center 3: ~2%)

Source: AI In Your Endoscopy Practice | Lines: 59–64

Additional caveats from the evidence base:

  • Algorithms detect more diminutive polyps — this is the primary driver of ↑ ADR in trials
  • Real-world performance gains are smaller than RCTs demonstrate
  • Risk of alert fatigue from false alarms during procedure

Take-home box: Algorithm performance ≠ real-world impact. The triple-blinded RCT design by Barkin is now the gold standard for evaluating AI in endoscopy — it reveals that availability ≠ adoption, and adoption drives outcomes.

2. "Will AI replace me or my trainees?"

Source: AI-Driven Gastroenterology | Lines: 351–364

The "descaling" concern is real and deserves serious attention:

  • **Air France Flight 447 **(2009): Autopilot disengaged at 37,000 feet; pilots could not recover manually because they had become dependent on automation
  • Pilots are now required to do manual flights periodically to maintain skills

Expert panel response:

  • Training programs should include withdrawals with AND without AI
  • Philosophical question raised: Is practicing without HD scopes "upskilling"? If trainees will never practice without AI once in independent practice, what is the baseline we're protecting?
  • Topol's concern: Learning with AI assistants may never develop a baseline skill set if always assisted
  • Counterpoint: Need certain competency level THEN use AI as enhancement — parallel to driving with backup cameras; you still learn basic skills first

Source: AI In Your Endoscopy Practice | Lines: 279–298

The "Centaur model" from Formula One offers a framework:

  • Human-AI collaboration for optimal performance
  • AI proposes constantly; human frames and decides
  • System learns from both machine and human feedback
  • Even 0.15 seconds matters for competitive advantage

The aviation industry model also provides guidance:

  • Assistive control systems for pilots with standardized checklists (computer-driven)
  • Black box data → continuous learning from every flight
  • Crew resource management ≠ human redundancy
  • System designed to reduce burden, not add to it

The radiology model shows another path:

  • AI triages and prioritizes cases; human makes final decision
  • Radiologists moved up the value chain → decision makers, not image finders
  • AI handles routine; humans handle complex cases

Take-home box: AI will change what it means to be a gastroenterologist, but the evidence points toward augmentation — not replacement. The Centaur (human + AI) outperforms either alone, but only when the human component is competent.

5. "How does AI affect fellowship curricula? Should trainees learn to code?"

Source: Artificial Intelligence and Precision Education in Gastroenterology | Lines: 387–424

The evolution of GI training paradigms:

  • **Time-based training **(pre-~2009): Time + experience = competence. Field expanded from 2 to 3 years due to complexity
  • **Competency-based medical education **(CBME, ~2012–present): Shift toward individualized competency progression
  • **Precision Medical Education **(PME, current interstitial phase): Right educational intervention for right learner at right time

Three fundamental problems with CBME:

  1. Conceptual complexity: Defining competence is simple in theory, complex in practice; hard to observe subtle forms of performance (e.g., PDLI variations)
  2. Psychometric/measurement problems: Competence is not binary; learning curves vary wildly; hard to judge individual competencies in real-time supervision
  3. Logistical problems: Attending-fellow interactions are intermittent; unsupervised training impractical due to Medicare/Medicaid constraints; huge administrative burden

**PME Framework **(AAMC Model)

  • Central to PME: Data about the learner — not snapshots, but longitudinal data (clinical experiences, competence measures, career goals)
  • AI transforms raw data into actionable insights; interventions developed based on those insights

The Four P's of PME:

  • Proactive: Anticipating needs before problems manifest
  • Personalized: Individual-focused interventions
  • Participatory: Learner engaged in the process
  • Predictive: AI-enabled pattern recognition for future risk

Funding scale: AMA grants — $12,000,000 total for 4-year projects covering clinical reasoning, communication skills, procedural skill certification, and coaching effectiveness

SECTION II: ORGAN/SYSTEM-SPECIFIC APPLICATIONS — THE EVIDENCE IN DETAIL

A. COLONOSCOPY — The Most Mature AI Application in GI

Real-Time Polyp Detection (CADe): The Evidence Base

Source: AI In Your Endoscopy Practice | Lines: 42–65

The largest evidence base for any AI application in endoscopy:

  • ~48 randomized controlled studies with over 40,000 patients
  • 5 FDA-approved products for colon polyp detection

Clinical impact from studies:

  • Normal detection rate ↑ 20%
  • Adenoma miss rate ↓ approximately 50%
  • Adenomas per colonoscopy ↑ significantly
  • Benefits apply to all providers regardless of experience level (not just low ADR performers)
  • No significant impact on withdrawal time

Caveats and limitations:

  • Algorithms detect more diminutive polyps → primary driver of ↑ ADR (question: is this clinically meaningful?)
  • Real-world performance gains are smaller than RCTs demonstrate
  • Risk of descaling (losing ability to detect without AI assistance)
  • Alert fatigue from false alarms

Real-World Impact: 3 Million Colonoscopies Across the U.S.

Source: AI Colonoscopy and Adenoma Detection | Lines: 10–96

Study design — the largest real-world AI impact study in GI:

  • Database: TriNetX U.S. Collaborative Network — federated EHR data from 67 healthcare organizations
  • Pre-AI era: January 2015 – December 2019 (~1,600,000 encounters)
  • AI era: January 2022 – June 2025 (~2,000,000 encounters)
  • Excluded 2020–2021 (COVID disruption + early adoption transition)
  • Population: Adults aged 45–90 years who underwent colonoscopy; excluded CRC diagnosis at or within 1 month of colonoscopy
  • 1:1 propensity score matching to balance covariates (demographics, comorbidities, indications, BMI) — standardized mean difference <0.1
  • Post-matching: both cohorts ~1,500,000 each

Primary outcomes:

Outcome Pre-AI Era AI Era Risk Ratio
Adenoma detection 1.8% 33.6% 1.97
Advanced adenoma detection 0.13% 0.19% 1.48
Interval CRC (6–36 months) 0.21% 0.11% 0.53 (47% reduction)

All results statistically significant. Key finding: more adenomas detected upstream → fewer interval cancers downstream.

Important limitations:

  • Ecological comparison (not direct AI vs. no-AI comparison) — other quality improvements occurred during this period
  • Time period includes: ↑ focus on withdrawal time, ↑ focus on clean bowel prep, ADR feedback programs
  • Reliance on ICD-10 diagnosis codes rather than direct pathologic review (less granular)
  • Within AI era, many CADe systems exist with different sensitivities and integration workflows
  • Unmeasured confounders: Endoscopist-level ADR metrics, procedure quality metrics, sedation type, prep adequacy

Conclusions:

  • Findings consistent with CADe trial literature showing higher ADR with CADe
  • Directional improvements across unselected multi-institutional real-world population (vs. controlled settings)
  • Most trials looked at adenoma detection but not interval cancer — this study fills that gap
  • Interpretation: improvements likely reflect broader advancement in endoscopy quality, not isolated CADe effect → hypothesis generating

Clinical vignette: A 56-year-old patient undergoes screening colonoscopy. In the pre-AI era, a flat diminutive adenoma in the transverse colon is missed. Three years later, they present with interval cancer. In the AI era, a real-time CADe system flags the lesion during withdrawal — it is resected. This scenario, repeated across millions of procedures, translates to the 47% interval CRC reduction seen in real-world data.

The Definitive Test: Triple-Blinded RCT of CADe Availability

Source: AI Colonoscopy and Adenoma Detection | Lines: 188–320

Background — the RCT vs. real-world discrepancy:

  • Controlled RCTs show ↑ ADR with CADe; pragmatic real-world trials often show no improvement
  • Possible explanations: alert fatigue, false positives reducing endoscopic thrust, Hawthorne effect (observed operators behave better), selection bias in RCT populations

Study design — a novel approach:

  • Triple-blinded, fully concealed design: Patients unaware of trial participation; endoscopy staff unaware of trial existence; data analysts blinded to allocation
  • This removes the Hawthorne effect from colonoscopy performance entirely
  • Randomization by room availability (not by use): Room with CADe vs. room without CADe
  • Preserves authentic clinical behavior post-CADe implementation — endoscopists free to use or not use CADe at their discretion
  • 1:1 randomization, stratified by center, endoscopist, patient sex, age group

Setting and population:

  • Three Quebec high-volume academic centers, 2-year period
  • Patients aged 45–89 undergoing screening/surveillance/diagnostic colonoscopy
  • Operators: Board-certified gastroenterologists and general surgeons (full-time)
  • Exclusions: Emergency colonoscopy, known/suspected polyposis, known IBD

Intervention: Medtronic GI Genius CADe system — activated by research staff each morning before team arrival; endoscopists free to deactivate temporarily or for rest of day at own discretion

**Results **(1,744 patients randomized)

  • Primary outcome — ADR: ITT analysis showed NO significant difference between groups; per-protocol analysis showed >5% absolute difference (significant when CADe was actually used)
  • CADe utilization rate: Only 57.8% overall (not even the expected 70%) — Center 1: ~70%, Center 2: <40%, Center 3: ~2%
  • Advanced lesion detection: Significantly improved in BOTH ITT and per-protocol analyses — the key finding that survived even incomplete adoption
  • All other secondary outcomes: No significant differences between groups

Conclusions and policy implications:

  • Triple-blinded concealed RCT is a reproducible framework for AI evaluation in GI endoscopy
  • Equipping rooms with CADe is necessary but NOT sufficient — incomplete adoption attenuates system-level benefit
  • Clinical AI success/failure depends on workflow integration + clinician adoption (not just algorithm performance)
  • Tailored implementation strategies required for clinical adoption; must address clinician uptake barriers beyond just equipment availability

Optical Diagnosis (CADx): "Resect and Discard" in Practice

Source: AI In Your Endoscopy Practice | Lines: 67–101

What CADx does:

  • Algorithms determine polyp histology in real-time during colonoscopy
  • Accuracy matches experts: approximately 85–90%
  • Displays real-time classification: neoplastic (adenoma) vs. hyperplastic on endoscopy monitor

Clinical application requirements:

  • Must visualize the polyp → algorithm provides diagnosis
  • Operator must agree/disagree with confidence level
  • Enables "resect and discard" or "diagnose and leave" strategies for diminutive polyps

Approved products: Multiple products undergoing FDA approval; European approvals already in place for several solutions (CE marking)

Advantages of CADx implementation:

  • Immediate decision on adenoma vs. hyperplastic polyp — no waiting for pathology
  • Hyperplastic polyps → can be left behind (↓ unnecessary polypectomies and associated risks)
  • High accuracy in clinical practice
  • Cost savings on pathology and equipment
  • Faster workflow: Patients can be told surveillance interval same-day without waiting for histopathology

Disadvantages and concerns:

  • Performance inconsistent under different circumstances (product location, image modality variations, operator experience levels)
  • Image quality is critical factor for accuracy
  • Risk of misclassification leading to missed adenomas
  • Risk of overreliance when blindly trusting AI diagnosis
  • Need for ongoing training with and without AI

The Upskilling Effect: Does CADx Make You Better?

Source: AI Colonoscopy and Adenoma Detection | Lines: 322–406

Study design — the first longitudinal study of CADx impact on skill:

  • **4-year prospective cohort **(2021–2025): Montreal University Hospital Center
  • CADx system: CatEye — available throughout study period; endoscopist choice whether to use on each case (not mandated)
  • 1,321 patients, 2,855 total polyps (CADx-assisted: 1,257 [44%]; unassisted: 1,598 [56%])
  • Mean polyp size: 7.25 mm; histology prevalence: adenoma ~60%, hyperplastic ~20%, serrated lesions ~6%
  • 30 endoscopists (17 trainees); all made at least one unassisted diagnosis; 60% made at least one CADx-assisted diagnosis
  • 4 endoscopists performed >75% of their procedures with CADx

Primary outcome — the upskilling signal:

  • CADx-assisted accuracy: ↑3.4% per month (statistically significant)
  • Unassisted accuracy: ↑1.1% per month (not statistically significant)
  • CADx-assisted curve: steep positive slope over 4 years; unassisted curve: flatter, plateauing around 65–70%

Secondary outcomes — all diagnostic metrics trending higher for CADx-assisted:

  • Specificity: +2.3% per month (CADx-assisted, significant)
  • Positive predictive value: +5% per month (CADx-assisted, significant)
  • Unassisted cases did not show these trends

Interpretation — the upskilling mechanism:

  • CADx provides immediate feedback during polyp evaluation (vs. delayed pathology results ~1 month)
  • Reinforces correct pattern recognition through: independent agreement with endoscopist's diagnosis; directing attention to cases of diagnostic uncertainty
  • Provides objective second opinion in real-time (rare without AI)

CADe vs. CADx — different behavioral effects:

  • CADe: Activated during withdrawal, automates detection (passive for endoscopist) — may carry descaling risk
  • CADx: Requires active participation (switch on system, adequately wash polyp, center in field of view) — this active engagement may explain upskilling effect

Limitations: Non-randomized design → significant selection bias risk; endoscopists free to choose CADx use case-by-case; cannot definitively attribute performance changes solely to CADx

Future research question: What happens to diagnostic performance if CADx is withdrawn after extended use? (The descaling test for CADx)

Take-home box: Over 4 years, optical diagnosis performance improved faster with CADx use. Endoscopists eventually surpassed mean unassisted performance. Findings support AI-enhanced learning hypothesis — but the descaling question remains open for CADx.

Why Lesions Are Missed — and How AI Addresses Each Cause

Five reasons lesions are missed:

  1. Hard to see — subtle morphology, poor lighting, suboptimal angle
  2. Technique, time, and fatigue — withdrawal speed, endoscopist experience level
  3. Lack of mucosal exposure — poor bowel prep hides lesions
  4. Not recognized → inattentional blindness phenomenon: lesion visible but not consciously registered
  5. Barrett's esophagus example: lesions relatively uncommon → easily overlooked; general endoscopists lack familiarity with presentation

Multimodal Large Language Models for Polyp Analysis

Source: AI Colonoscopy and Adenoma Detection | Lines: 409–500

Background: Multimodal LLMs (MLLMs) have shown impressive performance in radiology image understanding, histopathology analysis, automated report generation, and clinical decision support — but applications in gastroenterology/colonoscopy have been limited and complicated

**Study by Kyung Kim **(Seoul National University Hospital)

  • Datasets: Real Colon dataset + Sun dataset (annotated)
  • Two experimental tasks: (1) Polyp detection, (2) Polyp localization using bounding boxes
  • Compared against: Three internal medicine residents + three gastroenterologists

**In-context learning approach **(Gemini 2.5 Pro)

  • Varied number of example images: 0–128 shots from Sun dataset; evaluated on Real Colon dataset
  • Detection accuracy improved with more example shots: Zero-shot: 80% → 128 shots: nearly 90%
  • Key finding: Only 8 shots sufficient for meaningful improvement — practical without large-scale custom training
  • Performance approached internal medicine resident level

**Fine-tuning approach **(LLaMA 3.2 Vision + MedLLaMA)

  • Training: >24,000 annotated images from Sun dataset
  • Polyp detection accuracy: 58% → 93% after fine-tuning
  • Localization MAE: ~14% → 3% (↓ error)
  • Accuracy at IOU ≥0.5: 39% → 96%

Comparison with human doctors:

  • Gastroenterologists still showed higher accuracy than both internal medicine residents and Gemini (with in-context learning)
  • However, difference between gastroenterologists and Gemini was minimal
  • MLLM showed higher consistency across repeated trials than humans — valuable for clinical quality control

Clinical implications:

  • In-context learning: Practical without large-scale custom training; few examples (8 shots) enough for meaningful improvement
  • Fine-tuning: Highest accuracy, comparable to expert level; greater consistency than human endoscopists
  • Potential applications: Quality assurance in clinical endoscopy; training support where expert availability limited

Limitations: Current studies on static polyp images (not video); more complex real-time scenarios being developed; future work: 3D polyp perception and size estimation

Machine Learning for Polyp Risk Stratification Using VA Health Records

Source: AI Colonoscopy and Adenoma Detection | Lines: 503–611

**Study by Ethan Espinosa **(VA Boston Health Care System)

  • Database: VA Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW) — national repository of VHA clinical/administrative data
  • Study period: January 2010 – January 2025; patients with ≥2 colonoscopies ≥8 months apart
  • Initial patients from CDW: ~408,000 → final study cohort: ~32,000 patients (after exclusions for IBD, poor bowel prep, outliers, excessive missing information)

33 features extracted across three domains:

  • Personal data: Smoking history, alcohol use, family CRC history, demographics
  • Lab data: Blood panel values (triglycerides, mean platelet volume, etc.)
  • Pathology data: Raw pathology text from first colonoscopy

**NLP processing — BioBERT **(transformer-based model for biomedical text mining)

  • Challenge: Pathology reports are full text → cannot be processed by traditional ML models
  • Trained classifiers to extract: neoplastic polyp presence (yes/no), number of neoplastic polyps (count), location (proximal, rectosigmoid, or pan-colonic), subtype/grade (tubular adenoma → adenocarcinoma)

Five models tested: Logistic regression, XGBoost, decision tree, LightGBM, random forest

  • Training set: 25,000 patients; test set: 6,000 patients (unseen data)
  • 5-fold cross-validation with minimal variance (~0.01) across folds → supports generalizability

Results: All models: AUC 0.71–0.72 (consistent across models, better than chance)

SHAP analysis — feature importance:

  • Pathology findings ranked as most important features — 4 of top 10 SHAP features came from pathology reports: number of neoplastic polyps, presence of neoplastic polyp (grade), proximal location, pan-colonic involvement
  • 6 of top 10 from personal history and lab values — cumulative impact on prediction still significant
  • Directionality examples: Higher polyp count → positive SHAP value toward neoplasia; ≥2 polyps → higher impact; age ~60 years threshold for model influence; triglycerides in normal range (~100 mg/dL) → increased influence toward neoplasia prediction

Clinical application: Model can risk stratify follow-up colonoscopy timing — helps determine if patient should return at 7 years vs. 10 years; contextualizes patient's individual risk using comprehensive history

Limitations: 90% data exclusion (408,000 → 32,000) due to missing information and outlier values — potential for selection bias; however, changing strictness of exclusion criteria did not significantly affect model performance

4D HRM Metrics for EGJ Outflow Obstruction

Source: Esophageal Motility Disorders | Lines: 36–117; DDW 2024 Esophageal Motility | Lines: 50–93

Background: HRM is standard for Chicago Classification v4.0 esophageal motility diagnosis. EGJOO diagnosed on HRM alone is always clinically inconclusive — Chicago Classification requires additional confirmatory testing (FLIP, TBE).

4D HRM concept: Builds on standard HRM (time, space, pressure domains) and adds 4th metric: cross-sectional area derived from impedance — no additional procedures required (uses data already collected).

Derivation process: Manually identify 6 swallow landmarks per study; software delineates 4 phases: accommodation (esophagus relaxes for bolus), compartmentalization (bolus contained in esophageal body), peristalsis + esophageal emptying, ampullary emptying (complete distal clearance).

Three key 4D metrics:

  • **4D EGJ Distensibility Index **(DI) = max EGJ cross-sectional area ÷ intraluminal pressure during phase 3
  • 4D Max EGJ Diameter = maximum EGJ diameter achieved during phase 3
  • 4D Clearance Ratio = 1 – (post-peristalsis bolus volume ÷ pre-peristalsis volume); value closer to 0 = impaired emptying; closer to 1 = effective emptying

Study design: Retrospective observational cohort (Northwestern Medicine Motility Registry, 2014–2024); n = 123 patients with EGJOO on HRM + completed FLIP + TBE; mean age 59 years, 60% female; primary indication: dysphagia (89%).

Key findings:

  • Both 4D EGJ DI and 4D max EGJ diameter showed significant differences across FLIP EGJ opening classes; normal and borderline groups had greater values vs. reduced group (p<0.05)
  • 4D Clearance Ratio aligned with TBE outcomes; significant differences across TBE classes; TBE negative and intermediate groups had increased clearance ratios vs. TBE positive patients
  • Combined FLIP + TBE outcome analysis: All three 4D metrics significantly differed across combined outcomes

Extreme outcome separation — reliable rule-in/rule-out:

  • Zero patients with 4D EGJ max diameter <8 mm fell in negative FLIP+TBE class
  • Zero patients with 4D EGJ DI >2.1 AND diameter >10.1 fell in positive class
  • These results suggest reliable rule-in/rule-out at extremes

Intermediate class analysis: With abnormal EGJ metrics (diameter <8): 8/14 also had abnormal clearance ratio. With normal EGJ metrics (DI >2.1, diameter >10.1): 9/11 had normal clearance ratio. Multi-parameter approach enhances evaluation even in intermediate class.

Take-home box: 4D HRM metrics may reduce the need for multiple confirmatory procedures (FLIP, TBE) in EGJOO evaluation — extracting more diagnostic value from data already collected during standard HRM.

Cell-Free DNA Fragmentome for Non-Invasive Liver Disease Detection

Source: AI Applications in Liver Diseases and Transplantation | Lines: 452–557

Clinical problem: Incidence/mortality from liver disease increasing over two decades; ~1 in 4 US adults have undiagnosed MASLD. Liver disease exists on continuum: steatosis → fibrosis → cirrhosis → HCC; even among those with fibrosis, effective treatments exist. Among cirrhotics: lifetime HCC risk ~1 in 3; <20% of high-risk patients receive guideline-adherent liver cancer screening.

**Cell-free DNA **(cfDNA) DNA of nucleosomal origin, freely circulating after cell death; in cancer patients: small proportion directly from tumor. First-generation liquid biopsies looked for specific mutations → limited early-stage sensitivity.

Novel approach — cell-free DNA fragmentome: Full compendium of tens of millions of cfDNA fragments from <1mL blood; genome-wide fragment length/distribution reflects genomic changes, epigenomic changes, chromatin state alterations in cancer; assayed using inexpensive, low-coverage whole genome sequencing.

Computational approaches developed:

**DELFI **(DNA Evaluation of Fragments for Early Interception) Analyzes genome-wide length distributions of cfDNA fragments; healthy individuals: tightly regulated around 167 base pairs (nucleosome length); cancer: chaotic fragmentation profiles due to altered DNA packaging; uses short-to-long fragment ratio across genome.

**ARTEMIS **(Analysis of Repeat Elements in Disease) Looks at "dark genome" (LINEs, SINEs, transposable elements); historically excluded due to alignment challenges; alignment-free approach to reconstruct global repeat landscape; uses machine learning on combined fragmentation + repeat data.

Liver cancer detection results: Cross-validation across chromosomal arms (5 Mb bins); healthy individuals: very consistent fragment ratios; cancer patients: aberrant patterns throughout genome; model validated in external cohort from separate institution.

Extending to non-malignant liver disease: Signal present even in **cirrhosis **(pre-cancer). Study assembled with discovery and validation cohorts: healthy livers, early liver disease/fibrosis, advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis.

Fragmentome changes in liver disease: Fragments overall slightly shorter in cirrhosis vs. healthy; changes observed in fragmentation profiles, repeat landscape (transposable elements), distribution at epigenetic hotspots.

**Combined classifier performance **(DELFI + ARTEMIS)

  • External validation in separate institutional cohorts: Early liver disease/fibrosis detection: ~50%; advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis detection: >80%

Comparison with FIB4: FIB4 failed to detect early fibrosis in study cohorts; cfDNA fragmentome approach showed potential for earlier detection.

**Methylome analysis **(mechanism investigation) Whole methylome sequencing on cfDNA from subset of individuals; cell-type deconvolution revealed changes in immune cell cfDNA contribution and changes in liver-specific cell cfDNA contribution. Unmethylated fragments from altered cell types were shorter in cirrhosis; unique methylation positions mapped to regions enriched for short fragments in cirrhosis — confirms liver-specific signal in cfDNA.

Cross-reactivity testing: Evaluated model specificity in other fibrotic conditions (chronic pancreatitis, benign lung nodules, autoimmune/inflammatory processes) — low scores in these non-liver conditions → model is liver-specific.

Cancer vs. non-cancer signal differentiation: **Liver cancer model **(y-axis) elevated in malignancy, NOT elevated in fibrosis/cirrhosis; **liver disease model **(x-axis) elevated in both malignant AND non-malignant pathology — likely reflects fibrotic origins of many liver cancers.

Clinical implementation potential: cfDNA test can screen for fibrotic liver disease (earlier detection), route patients to appropriate care pathways, identify those needing liver cancer surveillance; complementary to existing fragmentomic blood test for cancer detection.

Future directions: Translation in diverse clinical settings underway; development for both cancer and non-malignant chronic conditions; ongoing validation across populations and healthcare systems.

Take-home box: The cfDNA fragmentome approach (DELFI + ARTEMIS) detects early liver fibrosis (~50%) and advanced cirrhosis (>80%) from <1mL blood — outperforming FIB4 which failed to detect early fibrosis. The liver cancer and liver disease signals are distinct, enabling separate screening pathways for malignancy vs. chronic fibrotic disease.

**Heat map analysis **(expanded) Improvement across every topic following tool utilization. Largest gains in initially lowest-scoring topics: Liver section: doubled following tool use; small bowel: ~20–30% ↑ post-tool; colorectal: ~20–30% ↑ post-tool.

Program benefits: Rapid integration of new guidelines; access to information not yet in traditional board prep material — critical for continued clinical success.

LLMs in Clinical Decision Support: The Experience Level Effect

Source: Artificial Intelligence and Precision Education in Gastroenterology | Lines: 257–377

Study background and gaps: LLMs posited to support clinicians' information needs and decision-making; real-world impacts on clinical settings and medical education remain unclear.

**Prior study reference **(Sunny Chung, Dennis Chung Team) Developed CDSS with customized LLM for UGI bleeding management called GutGPT (customized LLM). Findings: Users with LLM-enhanced CDSS had ↑ effort expectancy (ease of use); no significant difference in intention. Limitation: Did not examine knowledge or confidence in UGI bleeding management.

Current study objectives: Investigate effect of AI CDSS with vs. without GutGPT on: Users' knowledge of UGI bleeding management; confidence in that knowledge; adoption propensity of AI CDSS for UGI bleeding. Extended to examine experience level effects across physicians (PGY-2 through senior consultants) and medical students.

AI tool components:

  • Machine learning risk prediction dashboard: Patient-specific risk scores: 0 to 1 (higher = higher risk); shows number of similar patients requiring hospital-based intervention; charts indicating features contributing to risk prediction
  • **GutGPT **(customized LLM) Layered on top of ML dashboard; similar interface to ChatGPT; trained on UGI bleeding guidelines; responses supported with guideline references

**Study design **(PICO format)

  • Population: Medical students, physicians PGY-2 through senior consultants in UGI-facing specialties
  • Intervention arm: ML dashboard + GutGPT (LLM-enhanced); Comparator arm: ML dashboard only (without LLM)

Outcome variables: Adoption propensity (AI CDSS use intention); explainability perception (understanding factors/process behind recommendations); knowledge in UGI bleeding management; confidence in knowledge.

Simulation session procedure: Pre-simulation survey completion → randomization to GutGPT+dashboard OR dashboard-only arm → simulation session with 5 UGI bleeding scenarios (first three: admittance + scope decisions; post-survey phase 1; two additional: care management — medications, blood transfusion; post-survey phase 2; one-on-one interview).

Current enrollment status: Target: 100 participants; enrolled so far: 80 (ongoing study) — 42 physicians, 38 students; 42 in GutGPT arm, 38 in dashboard-only arm.

Statistical analysis method: Repeated measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA); assessed impact of pre/post simulation on outcomes; examined arm effects and experience level effects.

Knowledge and confidence results: Knowledge ↑ for both arms; confidence ↑ for both arms. Increase in knowledge more prominent in GutGPT arm (not statistically significant). Knowledge/confidence gains driven mostly by medical students; physicians showed ↑ confidence after simulation.

Adoption propensity results — differential by arm and experience:

  • AI CDSS use intention: Marginally ↑ only among students in dashboard-only arm; no increase among GutGPT-arm students; no increase among physicians
  • Explainability perception: Students in dashboard-only arm: ↑ explainability perception; physicians in GutGPT arm: ↑ explainability perception

Interpretation of results — the experience level effect:

  • Students preferred ML dashboard because: Specific risk scores (0–1) more translatable to clinical decisions; GutGPT caused cognitive overload and information uncertainty; students struggled to synthesize GutGPT responses into decisions; lower knowledge/experience made prompt formulation difficult
  • Physicians showed ↑ explainability perception in GutGPT arm because: Higher experience and knowledge enabled verification of LLM responses; one senior resident: "GutGPT gave pretty accurate answers, consistent with clinical judgment"; LLM confirmed their initial decisions

Key findings summary: AI CDSS (with LLM) enhances knowledge and confidence, especially for medical students; positive implications for medical education; adoption propensity varies by arm and experience level — LLM may cause cognitive overload in less experienced users.

Future research considerations: Durability of knowledge gains (follow-up at 3–6 months); subgroup analysis by physician seniority level; integration into EMR systems (currently standalone).

Q&A insights: Participants briefed on both dashboard and GutGPT before simulation; could use GutGPT whenever they wanted (not mandated); GutGPT trained only on UGI bleeding guidelines; GutGPT provides references from guidelines in responses.

Take-home box: The same AI tool can be helpful or harmful depending on user experience level. Less experienced users (students) benefited more from structured dashboards with specific risk scores; experienced clinicians could effectively leverage LLMs because they had the knowledge to verify accuracy. This has profound implications for how AI tools should be designed and deployed across different levels of clinical training.

Precision Medical Education Framework: The AAMC Model

Source: Artificial Intelligence and Precision Education in Gastroenterology | Lines: 380–479

Defining the problem — variable fellow needs:

  • **Juana **(PGY-1) Strong technical skills, weak clinical reasoning → ahead endoscopically, behind clinically
  • **Micah **(PGY-1) Strong clinical reasoning, weak technical skills → ahead clinically, behind endoscopically
  • Both frustrated despite same curriculum and timeline — the one-size-fits-all approach fails both

Evolution of GI training paradigms:

  • **Time-based training **(pre-~2009): Time + experience = competence; field expanded from 2 to 3 years due to complexity
  • **Competency-based medical education **(CBME, ~2012–present): Shift toward individualized competency progression
  • **Precision Medical Education **(PME, current interstitial phase): Right educational intervention for right learner at right time

Three fundamental problems with CBME:

  1. Conceptual complexity: Defining competence is simple in theory, complex in practice; hard to observe subtle forms of performance (e.g., PDLI variations); milestone products sometimes hard to apply practically
  2. Psychometric/measurement problems: Competence is not binary (not a single moment of achievement); learning curves vary wildly; hard to judge individual competencies in real-time supervision
  3. Logistical problems: Attending-fellow interactions are intermittent; unsupervised training impractical due to Medicare/Medicaid constraints; huge administrative burden

**PME Framework **(AAMC Model) Central to PME: Data about the learner — not snapshots, but longitudinal data (clinical experiences, competence measures, career goals). AI transforms raw data into actionable insights; interventions developed based on those insights.

The Four P's of PME:

  • Proactive: Anticipating needs before problems manifest
  • Personalized: Individual-focused interventions
  • Participatory: Learner engaged in the process
  • Predictive: AI-enabled pattern recognition for future risk

Funding and current research: AMA grants: $12,000,000 total for 4-year projects. Focus areas: Individual and team-based clinical reasoning; communication skills; procedural skill certification; coaching effectiveness.

Operationalizing PME with AI:

  • **Inputs **(data to collect) Procedural data (colonoscopy skills assessments); clinical data; knowledge measures; behavioral data
  • AI-supported analysis: Pattern recognition across all data points; identification of skill gaps and trajectories
  • Output: Targeted insights for individual fellows

AI's role in learner performance analysis: Aggregates data systematically; detects patterns humans might miss; compares fellows to standards or peers; identifies "learner phenotypes" (e.g., good technician vs. poor inspector).

Example — colonoscopy competency breakdown: Technical skill (scope control, polyp removal) vs. cognitive skill (mucosal inspection); AI can identify when fellow excels at one but not the other — enables specific coaching conversations.

Program-wide implementation model: (1) Curriculum + complementary measurements; (2) CCC (Clinical Competency Committee) for outcomes assessment; (3) AI-supported insights informing decisions; (4) Coaching and curriculum adjustments; (5) Program Evaluation Committee interventions.

Immediate actions for implementing PME: Identify priority use case (colonoscopy, endoscopy, clinic, QI); assess what data is currently collected; determine feasibility of new data collection; ensure institutional secure AI system access; engage stakeholders (faculty, fellows, staff); focus on learning (not just accreditation metrics).

Future opportunities: Milestone improvements and practical EPA development; innovative curricula and remediation strategies; multi-institutional collaboration and dissemination.

Key principle — human-AI partnership: AI informs decisions but humans make decisions; AI transforms data into insights, humans provide context and judgment; coaching and mentorship remain human responsibilities.

Q&A — mentor-learner interaction with AI:

  • Traditional apprenticeship learning (see one, do one, teach one) is valuable but unstructured; AI can complement by providing systematic data on learner competence
  • Still need human mentorship for synthesis and judgment

Learning to work with AI:

  • AI is becoming a competence for physicians (tool, product, subject)
  • Learners need to develop ability to interact with and provide feedback to AI systems — co-piloting skills increasingly important

Collecting continuous quality data:

  • Workplace-based assessments are challenging for faculty to complete; straight-lining of assessment forms common
  • Solutions: Timely, focused assessments (e.g., procedure-specific skills forms); voice-recorded verbal summaries at end of day; brief QR-code-based feedback systems

Real-time AI during procedures: Shared understanding of what feedback is needed; pick 1–2 competencies per session to evaluate; develop intention for the day/session; strategic instruments (e.g., QR codes) to make feedback efficient.

Learner-driven precision education: Fellows want data on their own performance; dashboards comparing to standards or peers can support self-reflection; self-assessment must be tied to external feedback (not just reflection alone); coaching conversations can use dashboard data for development planning.
