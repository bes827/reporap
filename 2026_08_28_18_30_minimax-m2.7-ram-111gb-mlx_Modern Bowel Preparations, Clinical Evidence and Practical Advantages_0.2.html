Modern Bowel Preparations, Clinical Evidence and Practical Advantages
  • Source: Alimentary Pharmacology & Therapeutics, 2026; 63(Suppl. 1):S12–S17
  • Topic: Colonoscopy bowel preparation efficacy, safety, and patient adherence
  • Author(s): Abraham Z. Cheloff, Aasma Shaukat (NYU Grossman School of Medicine)
1. Introduction
  • Colorectal cancer --> 2nd leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally
  • Colonoscopy = gold standard for colorectal cancer screening
  • High-quality colonoscopy depends on adequate bowel preparation
  • Inadequate prep --> repeat exam within 1 year
  • USMSTF guidelines highlight adequate prep need but provide no regimen selection guidance
1.1 Quality Metrics for Colonoscopy
  • Adenoma detection rate (ADR)
  • Sessile serrate lesion detection rate (SSLDR)
  • Cecal intubation rate
  • Adequacy of bowel preparation --> new priority indicator in recent quality metrics publication
2. Bowel Preparation Regimens Overview
  • Three categories based on volume:
  • High volume (4 L)
  • Low volume (2 L)
  • Ultra-low volume (<2 L)
2.1 High Volume Preparations
  • GoLYTELY (Azurity Pharmaceuticals) --> 4-L PEG-electrolyte solution
  • NuLYTELY (Azurity Pharmaceuticals) --> 4-L PEG, sulphate-free form
  • Contains: PEG-3350, sodium chloride, potassium chloride, buffering salts
  • Mechanism: iso-osmotic agent --> cleanses colon without fluid/electrolyte shifts
  • Standard label dosing: drink all 4 L until complete
  • Common split-prep: 2 L day before + 2 L morning of procedure
  • NuLYTELY has less salty taste and includes flavour packets
  • Side effects: nausea, bloating, abdominal cramping, difficulty tolerating large volume
  • Serious adverse events: <1% (arrhythmias, seizures) --> risk higher in patients with underlying risk factors
2.2 Low Volume Preparations
  • MoviPrep (Velinor AG) --> 2-L PEG-based bowel prep
  • Contains: PEG-3350, sodium sulphate, sodium chloride, potassium chloride, ascorbic acid, sodium ascorbate
  • Ascorbate components --> osmotic agents + electrolyte balance
  • Requires additional clear liquids (volume varies by dosing regimen)
  • Side effects: nausea, vomiting, abdominal bloating, electrolyte disturbances, dehydration
  • Contraindicated in G6PD deficiency (due to sodium ascorbate/ascorbic acid) --> can cause hemolysis
2.3 Ultra-Low Volume Preparations
  • Requires <2 L of purgative ingestion
2.3.1 Plenvu (1-L PEG Regimen)
  • PEG-based with same components as MoviPrep
  • Contains: PEG-3350, sodium sulphate, sodium chloride, potassium chloride, ascorbic acid, sodium ascorbate
  • Administered in two doses
  • Requires additional clear liquids for optimal cleansing
  • Side effects: dehydration, electrolyte abnormalities in 1%–2% of patients
  • Particularly affects: sodium and potassium levels
2.3.2 SUPREP (Oral Sulfate Solution)
  • Active ingredients: sodium sulphate, potassium sulphate, magnesium sulphate
  • Mechanism: strong osmotic effect --> promotes bowel-cleansing
  • Hypertonic solution
  • ↑ risk of electrolyte abnormalities in patients with underlying renal or cardiac disease
2.3.3 CLENPIQ (Sodium Picosulphate-Based)
  • Ingestion: 12 oz purgative + 2.5 L water
  • Often split into two doses
  • Contains: sodium picosulphate, magnesium oxide, citric acid
  • Mechanism: stimulates colonic motility + osmotic-based cleansing
  • Hypertonic solution
  • Not recommended for patients with underlying renal or cardiac disease
2.3.4 SUTAB (Tablet-Based)
  • Dosing: 24 tablets split into two doses + 2 L water
  • Active ingredients: sodium sulphate, magnesium sulphate, potassium chloride
  • Side effect profile similar to SUPREP
2.3.5 OsmoPrep (Discontinued)
  • Dosing: 32 tablets + 2 L water, often split into two doses
  • No longer available
  • Risks: dehydration, marked electrolyte disturbances
  • Risk of acute phosphate nephropathy in <1% of cases
  • Susceptible individuals: increased age, hypovolaemia, increased bowel transit time, active colitis, kidney disease, medications affecting renal perfusion
3. Comparative Review of Efficacy, Tolerability and Safety
3.1 PEG-Based Regimens
  • Isotonic solutions --> minimize fluid/electrolyte shifts
  • Appropriate for patients with significant medical comorbidities
3.1.1 High-Volume vs Low-Volume Comparison
  • 4-L split-dose PEG (e.g., GoLYTELY, NuLYTELY) --> considered standard historically
  • Meta-analysis of 17 RCTs showed:
  • High-volume split-dose (>3 L) = equivalent bowel cleanse to lower-volume regimens
  • No statistical difference between groups
  • Low heterogeneity between studies
  • However: high-volume preps substantially less tolerated
  • Patients report higher willingness to complete low-volume regimens in future
3.1.2 Low-Volume PEG Options
  • MoviPrep (2-L PEG + ascorbate) --> non-inferior to 4-L PEG regimens
  • SUFLAVE (2-L PEG with sodium sulfate, potassium chloride, magnesium sulfate, sodium chloride) --> non-inferior to 4-L PEG
  • Heterogeneity among study results:
  • Some studies: significantly ↑ efficacy with 4-L regimen
  • Some studies: ↑ efficacy with low-volume regimen
  • Low-volume regimens --> higher tolerability and compliance
  • Ascorbate-based preps: contraindicated in patients with underlying renal or cardiac disease
  • Limitation: 4-L regimen does not have this contraindication
3.1.3 Ultra-Low Volume PEG (Plenvu)
  • 1-L PEG + ascorbate regimen
  • Studies mainly compared to 2-L MoviPrep
  • Results show:
  • Non-inferiority demonstrated
  • ↑ cleansing efficacy vs other regimens (including right colon)
  • Associated with ↑ rates of emesis
  • Overall safety and tolerability comparable to other PEG regimens
  • ADR remained similar despite ↑ bowel-cleansing
  • Limitation: Only 1 of 9 studies in meta-analysis used 4-L comparator
  • Sparse data comparing ultra-low to high-volume options
3.2 Non-PEG-Based Regimens
  • Oral sulphate-based regimens (SUPREP, SUTAB) studied as alternatives to PEG
3.2.1 Oral Sulfate Solutions vs PEG
  • Meta-analysis of 7 studies showed:
  • No difference in bowel preparation quality between regimens
  • ↑ nausea and vomiting with sulphate solutions
  • Concern for use in populations predisposed to nausea/vomiting:
  • Diabetes patients
  • Gastroparesis patients
  • Foregut motility disorders
3.2.2 Sodium Picosulphate Regimens (CLENPIQ)
  • Two meta-analyses published in 2016:

Meta-analysis 1 (25 RCTs):

  • No difference in satisfactory bowel prep between picosulphate and PEG
  • Much heterogeneity in comparator groups (high-volume PEG, low-volume PEG, split-dose, full-dose)
  • Effects trended toward better bowel cleanse with PEG-based regimens
  • Patients more likely to complete picosulphate regimen
  • More agreeable to completing that prep in future

Meta-analysis 2:

  • Slightly ↑ bowel prep with picosulphate vs PEG
  • Only 4 studies pooled in analysis
3.2.3 Safety Considerations for Non-PEG Regimens
  • Sodium picosulphate: contraindicated in severe cardiac or renal disease
  • ↑ risk of hyponatremia especially in older adults
3.3 Limitations of Data
  • Major limitation: heterogeneity of study designs
  • Patient populations vary across studies
  • Dosing schedules inconsistent
  • Outcome measures differ
  • Comparison issues:
  • Low-volume vs traditional high-volume PEG (GoLYTELY)
  • Ultra-low vs 2-L products (MoviPrep)
  • Sparse comparative data between low-volume regimens themselves
  • Difficult to determine if one modern low-volume option is superior to another
  • Meta-analyses pool studies with:
  • Varying definitions of adequate preparation
  • Different cleansing scales
  • Inconsistent split-dose timing
  • Variable reporting on tolerability and safety
  • Results: significant clinical and statistical heterogeneity
  • Precision and generalizability of conclusions reduced
3.4 Patient Preferences
  • Key principle: maximize patient adherence
  • Best bowel prep = the one patient is willing to take completely
3.4.1 Volume Preferences
  • Low-volume, flavoured solutions preferred by patients
  • Split-dose timing critically shapes satisfaction
3.4.2 Taste and Flavouring
  • Unpleasant taste = consistent barrier to completing preparation
  • Flavour-enhanced regimens --> improved patient acceptance
3.4.3 Dosing Schedule Preferences
  • Patients prefer split-dose over single-dose regimens
  • Half evening before + half morning of colonoscopy
  • Split-dosing benefits:
  • Less bloating
  • Less nausea and vomiting
  • Personalized timing improves compliance
3.4.4 Formulation Preferences
  • Tablet formulations (e.g., SUTAB) emerging as promising alternatives
  • Patients prefer tablets over liquid due to:
  • Ease of ingestion
  • Reduced nausea
3.5 Real-World Considerations
  • Barriers to low-volume preparation:
  • Availability at health plans/pharmacies
  • Out-of-pocket costs for patients
3.5.1 Cost Barriers
  • Medicare/Medicaid cover the 4-L preparation
  • Patients who may benefit most from low-volume preps often face cost barriers
  • Older patients
  • Those with comorbidities associated with poor preparation
  • Advocacy and education needed at local, regional, national levels
3.5.2 Recommendations
  • Health plans should carefully consider covered population
  • Offer at least one low-volume preparation in addition to 4-L preparations
3.6 Role of Education and Navigation
  • Instructions/support provided = as important as agent selected
3.6.1 Best Practices for Patient Education
  • Provide instructions in patient's preferred/spoken language
  • Provide instructions at least 2 times between ordering and procedure date
  • Example: at clinic visit + 2 days prior to colonoscopy
  • Use simple language (ideally grade 4 reading level)
  • Provide instructions in at least 2 ways: written + verbal, text, or video
  • Use videos to educate patients
  • Send reminders (voice calls + text based on adherence)
  • Use navigators or educators
  • Consider offering preparation education classes (in-person or virtual)
3.6.2 Quality Improvement Strategies
  • Periodically survey patients for clarity and content of education materials
  • Make updates based on feedback
  • Consider use of electronic preparation navigation tools (standalone or EHR-integrated)
  • Develop separate instructions for special populations:
  • Prior failed bowel preparation
  • Spinal cord injury
  • Cystic fibrosis
  • Other specific conditions
4. Summary of Bowel Preparation Interventions
  • Adequate bowel prep --> fundamental to high-quality, cost-effective CRC screening via colonoscopy
4.1 Efficacy and Safety Summary
  • Multiple FDA-approved options available
  • Current options compare favourably in efficacy and safety
  • Tolerability: higher with low and ultra-low volume preparations
4.2 Key Factors in Selection
  • Patient preference = key factor in adherence
  • Cost = barrier to selecting bowel preparation
4.3 Maximizing Adherence Strategies
  • Instructions in patient's preferred language
  • Use reminders and navigation
  • Many available and emerging navigation tools to maximize bowel preparation quality
