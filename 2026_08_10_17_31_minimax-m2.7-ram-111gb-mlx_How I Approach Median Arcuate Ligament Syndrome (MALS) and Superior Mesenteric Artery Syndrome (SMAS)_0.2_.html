How I Approach Median Arcuate Ligament Syndrome (MALS) and Superior Mesenteric Artery Syndrome (SMAS)
  • Source: American Journal of Gastroenterology, Vol. Ahead of Print
  • Topic: Abdominal Compression Syndromes - Diagnostic Approach and Management
  • Author(s): Spiritos Z, Shouhed D, Cutchins A
Overview of Abdominal Compression Syndromes
  • MALS and SMAS are underrecognized abdominal compression syndromes
  • Symptoms overlap with common motility disorders and disorders of brain-gut interaction (DBGI)
  • Often misdiagnosed as functional dyspepsia or irritable bowel syndrome
  • May be attributed to eating disorders → delays diagnosis
  • Patients can go years without diagnosis undergoing repeated testing and empiric trials
  • Early recognition enables targeted intervention and nutritional recovery
Median Arcuate Ligament Syndrome (MALS)
Definition and Anatomy
  • Median arcuate ligament is a fibrous arch formed by convergence of right and left diaphragmatic crura
  • Compression involves the celiac artery and surrounding nerve plexus
  • Ligament becomes ptotic → compresses celiac structures
  • Commonly seen in connective tissue disorders (e.g., Ehlers-Danlos syndrome)
Pathophysiology
  • Historically described as celiac artery compression disorder
  • Growing evidence supports a neurogenic mechanism
  • Underlying pathophysiology remains incompletely understood
  • Mesenteric circulation provides substantial collateral flow → isolated ischemia unlikely
  • Relieving vascular compression alone (dividing ligament) often fails to provide durable relief
Clinical Presentation
  • Postprandial epigastric pain appearing shortly after eating/drinking
  • Pain onset: within minutes of food/beverage consumption
  • Character: squeezing or pressure-like pain (similar to crushing sensation of a vice grip)
  • Radiation: along left subcostal margin or to the back
  • Worsening factors:
  • Physical activity
  • Deep exhalation (upward diaphragmatic movement increases ligament tension)
  • Over time → sitophobia and progressive weight loss
  • Resembles functional dyspepsia early in disease course
Red Flags for MALS
  • Pain within minutes of eating/drinking
  • Lack of response to functional dyspepsia treatment
  • Progressive weight loss
  • Comorbid connective tissue hypermobility
Diagnostic Evaluation
Step 1: Exclusion of Common Foregut Pathology
  • Rule out peptic ulcer disease
  • Rule out gastroparesis (gastric emptying study)
  • Delayed emptying may represent associated finding in hypermobility patients with dysautonomia
  • May not be primary driver of symptoms
Step 2: Vascular Imaging (When Suspicion Remains High)
  • CT angiography with inspiratory and expiratory phases
  • Identifies signs of celiac artery compromise:
  • Focal narrowing with characteristic hooked configuration
  • Respiratory-dependent caliber changes
  • Post-stenotic dilatation
  • Mesenteric duplex ultrasound
  • Evaluates hemodynamic relationship between median arcuate ligament and celiac artery
  • Expiratory peak systolic velocity >200 cm/s = abnormal
  • Peak systolic velocity >350 cm/s = more specific for MALS
Important Caveats on Imaging
  • Radiographic celiac artery compression can be seen in asymptomatic individuals
  • Findings must always be interpreted in context of clinical presentation
Step 3: Functional Testing (When Vascular Imaging Inconclusive)
  • Celiac plexus block is primary diagnostic tool
  • Delivers local anesthetic + corticosteroid for more durable effect
  • Therapeutic effect is transient (usually lasting days to weeks)
  • Near-complete resolution of postprandial pain supports diagnosis
  • Helps predict which patients will benefit from surgery
Management
Nutritional Stabilization
  • Patients frequently do not meet caloric needs at presentation
  • Nasojejunal feeding (post-pyloric enteral) provides nutritional rehabilitation
  • Bypasses neural triggers → makes feeding less painful
  • Parenteral nutrition reserved for patients who cannot tolerate enteral support
Definitive Surgical Treatment
  • Surgical division of median arcuate ligament
  • Concurrent resection of celiac ganglion (ganglionectomy)
  • Division of ligament alone → associated with high failure rates
  • Combined ganglionectomy outcomes:
  • Improvement in roughly 85–90% of patients
  • Durable success in approximately 65–70% at five years (single-center series)
Postoperative Course and Complications
  • Most common complication: transient diarrhea (related to celiac plexus denervation)
  • Patients often not free of digestive symptoms after neurectomy
  • Does not necessarily indicate surgical failure
  • Many have comorbid conditions:
  • Dysautonomia
  • Mast cell activation
  • Concomitant compression disorders (SMAS, pelvic venous disease)
  • Longitudinal, multidisciplinary follow-up is important
  • Distinguishing inadequate decompression from associated pathology
Superior Mesenteric Artery Syndrome (SMAS)
Definition and Anatomy
  • Third portion of duodenum compressed between superior mesenteric artery (SMA) and aorta
  • Retroperitoneal fat pad normally maintains adequate separation between these structures
Pathophysiology
  • Conditions that reduce protective fat pad narrow the aortomesenteric (AOM) angle
  • Precipitates the syndrome
  • Causes:
  • Connective tissue laxity
  • Congenital predisposition
  • Acquired factors (most common: significant weight loss)
  • MALS is a frequent upstream contributor to SMAS development
Clinical Presentation
  • Distinct pattern from duodenal distention proximal to obstruction
  • Pain timing: 1–3 hours after eating (vs. immediate postprandial in MALS)
  • Pain location: periumbilical
  • Associated symptoms:
  • Nausea
  • Vomiting
  • Early satiety
  • Provoking foods: Bulky or high-residue foods:
  • Raw vegetables
  • Fibrous fruits
  • Coarse grains
  • Tough meats
  • Symptoms cause food aversion → drives further weight loss → worsens compression (vicious cycle)
Diagnostic Evaluation
Step 1: Exclusion of Luminal Disease
  • Upper endoscopy rules out mucosal pathology and gastric outlet obstruction
Step 2: Cross-Sectional Imaging
  • CT with IV contrast
  • Permits measurement of AOM angle and distance
  • Diagnostic thresholds:
  • Angle <18 degrees raises suspicion (normal: 28–65 degrees)
  • Distance <8 mm raises suspicion (normal: 10–28 mm)
Important Caveats on Imaging
  • Narrowed aortomesenteric angle can be observed in asymptomatic individuals
  • Particularly common in thin patients
  • Radiographic measurements must be interpreted alongside compatible clinical presentation
Step 3: Upper Gastrointestinal Series
  • Functional study evaluating contrast passage through duodenum
  • Specific but not sensitive
  • Becomes abnormal only when compression causes contrast retention
Management
Conservative Measures
  • Therapeutic goal: Restore retroperitoneal fat pad through nutritional support
  • Nasojejunal feeding bypasses extrinsic compression → often necessary to meet caloric needs
  • Parenteral nutrition reserved for patients who cannot tolerate enteral feeds
  • Conservative treatment success rate: only 10–30% of cases
Limitations of Conservative Management
  • Long-term compression can produce irreversible duodenal narrowing
  • Impaired motility persists despite nutritional rehabilitation
  • Most patients require surgical intervention
Surgical Intervention
  • Required when obstructive physiology persists despite conservative management
  • Duodenojejunostomy = most common surgical approach
  • Results are variable
  • Intestinal derotation with duodenoduodenostomy
  • Promising but still novel approach
  • Long-term follow-up data lacking
Postoperative Course
  • Patients often have arduous postoperative course
  • Underlying causes of difficulty:
  • Connective tissue disorders
  • Mast cell activation
  • Dysautonomia
  • Common issues:
  • Persistent nausea and abdominal pain
  • Delayed return of motility
  • Frequently requires temporary gastric decompression and jejunal feeding
  • Recovery ranges from several weeks to several months
  • Realistic expectations should be discussed before surgery
Comparison: MALS vs. SMAS vs. Functional Dyspepsia
Primary Mechanism
  • MALS: Compression of celiac plexus with/without celiac artery involvement
  • SMAS: Mechanical compression of duodenum between SMA and aorta
  • Functional Dyspepsia: Disorder of gut-brain interaction
Symptom Timing After Eating
  • MALS: Minutes
  • SMAS: 1–3 hours
  • Functional Dyspepsia: Minutes to one hour
Pain Location
  • MALS: Epigastric
  • SMAS: Periumbilical
  • Functional Dyspepsia: Epigastric
Predominant Symptom Drivers
  • MALS: Postprandial neural stimulation
  • SMAS: Duodenal distension proximal to obstruction
  • Functional Dyspepsia: Impaired gastric accommodation, visceral hypersensitivity, duodenal microinflammation
Food Triggers
  • MALS: Any solid food or liquid
  • SMAS: Bulky or high-residue foods (raw vegetables, fibrous fruits, coarse grains, tough meats)
  • Functional Dyspepsia: Large meals and spicy or high-fat foods
Weight Loss
  • MALS: Common
  • SMAS: Common
  • Functional Dyspepsia: Rare and usually mild
Effect of Exertion or Deep Exhalation
  • MALS: Often worsens symptoms
  • SMAS: No consistent effect
  • Functional Dyspepsia: No consistent effect
Associated Conditions
  • MALS: Hypermobility, dysautonomia
  • SMAS: Hypermobility, dysautonomia, rapid weight loss, scoliosis surgery
  • Functional Dyspepsia: Anxiety, depression
Clinical Decision Pathway Summary
When to Suspect Compression Syndromes
  • Postprandial epigastric pain with/without weight loss
  • Pain within minutes of eating/drinking
  • Lack of response to functional dyspepsia treatment
  • Comorbid hypermobility or connective tissue disorders
Evaluation Steps

1. Exclude common GI pathology (PUD, gastroparesis)

2. Obtain dynamic CT angiography and mesenteric duplex ultrasound

3. If imaging inconclusive → perform celiac plexus block

4. Near-complete pain resolution supports surgical candidacy

Key Differentiators from Functional Dyspepsia
  • Timing: Immediate postprandial (MALS) vs. 1–3 hours (SMAS)
  • Weight loss: Prominent in compression syndromes
  • Effect of exertion/deep exhalation: Worsens MALS symptoms
  • Comorbid hypermobility: Strong association with compression syndromes
Key Clinical Takeaways
Diagnostic Principles
  • Symptom timing and reproducibility are critical distinguishing features
  • Weight loss in context of GI symptoms should prompt evaluation for compressive pathology
  • Comorbid connective tissue hypermobility provides important clinical clue
  • Imaging findings must always be correlated with clinical presentation
Treatment Principles
  • Nutritional stabilization is essential before and during treatment
  • Nasojejunal feeding bypasses neural triggers and provides rehabilitation
  • Surgery for MALS requires ligament division + ganglionectomy (not ligament alone)
  • SMAS surgery required when conservative measures fail
  • Both conditions require multidisciplinary team (gastroenterology, surgery, nutrition)
Prognosis
  • MALS: 85–90% improvement with surgery; 65–70% durable success at 5 years
  • SMAS: Conservative treatment → only 10–30% success; surgery often necessary
  • Both represent treatable causes of severe GI morbidity when recognized early
Multidisciplinary Care
  • Patient selection is critical and best made within a multidisciplinary team
  • Strongest surgical candidates have:
  • Compatible symptom pattern
  • Corroborating imaging findings
  • Exclusion of more common pathology
  • Longitudinal follow-up essential due to comorbid conditions and persistent symptoms
