The Rise of 'Recruitmentology': Clinical Research, Racial Knowledge, and the Politics of Inclusion and Difference
  • Source: Social Studies of Science 38/5 (October 2008)
  • Topic: Clinical trial recruitment policies and racial knowledge production
  • Author(s): Steven Epstein, University of California, San Diego
1. Inclusion-and-Difference Paradigm
  • US federal policies mandating inclusion of underrepresented groups in clinical research
  • Dual mandate --> inclusion of members + measurement of differences by sex, race, ethnicity, age
  • 1993 NIH Revitalization Act --> required women and minorities in federally funded studies
  • FDA removed restrictions on women's participation (1993)
  • 1997 --> pediatric exclusivity extensions + NIH child inclusion policy
  • FDA rules on geriatric drug labeling and demographic subgroup tabulation
1.1 Policy Enforcement Mechanisms
  • NIH grant applications --> require numerical targets by sex/gender, race, ethnicity
  • 2005 NIMH policy --> researchers must specify recruitment milestones in advance
  • Agencies reserve right to suspend, terminate, or withhold funding for non-compliance
1.2 Institutional Backing
  • DHHS created specialized research networks:
  • Resource Centers for Minority Aging Research
  • Pediatric Pharmacology Research Units
  • National Centers of Excellence in Women's Health
2. The Body Hunt (Recruitment Challenges)
  • Finding appropriate participants --> major challenge in clinical research
  • Recruitment costs --> $500 million+ spent annually on mass media promotion
  • Average cost per participant (2001) --> $2,500 for screening/recruitment
  • 57% of clinical trials ran 1+ months late due to recruitment delays (2001)
  • Delays --> reduced profit time before patent expiration
2.1 Professionalization of Recruitment
  • Private companies increasingly recruited participants:
  • 1992 --> 7 million participants via private companies
  • 2001 --> 20 million via private companies
  • Growth of contract research organizations (CROs)
  • 2005 --> Anaclim launched as first minority-specific recruitment company
2.2 Competitive Pressures
  • Pediatric exclusivity extensions --> created "frenzy" for child subjects
  • Quote: "If you have a hypertensive kid, hold on to him. He'll be in hot demand" (FDA's Robert Temple)
3. Recruitmentology Defined
  • Term coined by author for empirical studies evaluating recruitment efficacy
  • Evaluates social, cultural, psychological, technological, economic means of convincing people to become/remain subjects
  • "Hard" recruitmentology --> scientific/experimental approaches
  • "Soft" recruitmentology --> advice literature, lessons learned
3.1 Hybrid Science Characteristics
  • Imports frameworks from sociology, anthropology, psychology, economics into clinical research
  • Clinical researchers --> act as "practical sociologists"
  • Generates knowledge about medically underserved communities
3.2 Recruitment Methods Studied
  • Primary care physician referrals
  • Hospital/clinic-based recruitment
  • Newspaper, radio, television advertising
  • Public notices, billboards, transit signs
  • Flyers, brochures, doorknob packets
  • Online database listings (ClinicalTrials.gov)
  • Direct mail, random-digit-dialing, cold calling
  • Celebrity campaigns
  • Community outreach (health fairs, churches, nursing homes)
3.3 Recruitmentology Sub-Genres
3.1.1 Barrier Identification Studies
  • Surveys to assess motivations for participation or refusal
  • Focus groups on attitudes toward research in specific communities
  • Common themes: distrust of researchers, lack of information, fear of experimentation, perceived lack of benefits
3.1.2 Cultural Analysis Studies
  • Examines cultural properties of targeted groups
  • Examples:
  • Confidentiality concerns in rural/small-town settings
  • Cultural meanings of blood --> affects willingness to provide samples
  • "Family orientation" of Indian subjects impacts enrollment
  • Warning --> reliance on simple ethnicity constructs may blind researchers to within-group differences
3.1.3 Network/Social Relation Studies
  • Researchers map webs of social relations to understand recruitment contexts
  • Example: Philip Gorelick's community network diagrams showing churches, PTAs, NIH connections
3.1.4 Cost-Benefit Analysis Studies
  • Compare costs of different recruitment strategies to "yield" (ratio enrolled vs. screened)
  • Example: Diabetes Prevention Program --> 3,800+ participants, nearly half people of color
  • African Americans --> more likely recruited via direct mail
  • American Indians --> community screening events most effective
4. Two Conceptions of Race in Recruitmentology
  • Tension between social actor conception vs. census category conception
4.1 Social Actor Conception
  • Group imagined as having interests, representatives, collective memory
  • Must be engaged as interlocutor
  • Example: researchers invoke "African American community" beliefs
4.2 Census Category Conception
  • Reduction to formal racial identifiers (required by NIH/FDA):
  • American Indian or Alaska Native
  • Asian
  • Black or African American
  • Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander
  • White
  • Hispanic/Latino vs. Not Hispanic/Latino
  • Subgroup comparisons --> mean differences reported as categorical differences
5. Trust and Mistrust in Recruitment
  • Collective memory of racism --> major barrier to recruitment
  • Tuskegee Syphilis Study (1932-1972) --> primary reference point for African Americans
5.1 Tuskegee Impact Studies
  • Detroit survey --> 50% of African Americans vs. 17% white respondents reported less trust in researchers due to Tuskegee
  • National telephone survey findings:
  • 42% of African Americans vs. 23% overall population --> distrust physician explanations about research
  • 25% of African Americans vs. 8% overall --> believe physician gave treatment without consent
  • Differences persisted after adjusting for social class
5.2 Historical Roots Beyond Tuskegee
  • Mistrust predates single events --> extends back to slavery and continues today
  • Examples cited by researchers:
  • Medical experimentation on slaves (antebellum South)
  • Black cadavers used for dissection (19th century)
  • "Night doctors" kidnapping blacks for experiments
  • Forced sterilization programs
  • Tuskegee + HIV/AIDS + crack cocaine in Black neighborhoods
5.3 Other Groups Affected
  • Latino communities --> similar patterns of exploitation
  • Example: Los Angeles 1989 --> African American/Latino infants received unlicensed Edmonston-Zagreb measles vaccine without parental consent
  • Native Americans --> confidentiality violations after syphilis studies
6. Trust-Building Strategies
6.1 Basic Approaches
  • Working through community gatekeepers (clergy, politicians)
  • Employing bilingual/multilingual staff
  • "Identity matching" --> front-line employees from studied group (efficacy not always confirmed)
  • Alliances between predominantly white universities and historically black institutions
6.2 Power Imbalance Recognition
  • Researchers advised to address duration of commitment
  • "Drive-by research" --> disappearing after trial completion (criticized)
  • Alternative: bring benefits back to community post-research
6.3 Cynical Half-Measures (Warnings)
  • "Black face" display at community forums --> tokenism
  • Example: researcher asked to recruit without knowing study design or purpose
6.4 Participatory Models
  • "Community-based participatory research" (CBPR) --> NIH funded since 2004/2007
  • Definition: "scientific inquiry conducted in communities and in partnership with researchers"
  • Requires deference to community authority
  • Example: Alaskan Native study (15 remote villages) --> continuous input from tribal leaders, community recruiters, Indigenous physicians on steering committee
6.5 Barriers to Implementation
  • Who defines "community"?
  • Who serves as representatives?
  • Participatory research doesn't automatically equalize power
  • May be used merely as tactic to obtain bodies
7. Globalization of Clinical Research
  • From 1987 --> FDA allowed drug approval applications based solely on foreign data
  • 1995-1999 --> 35% of trials included foreign sites (~1,140/year)
  • CROs especially likely to move operations offshore
  • Target countries: India, Uganda, Thailand, China, Indonesia
7.1 Why Global South?
  • Reduced research costs
  • "Pharmacologically naive" patients (no prior drug exposure)
  • Less stringent review boards
  • Less demanding patients
7.2 India as Case Study
  • Financial Times (2003) --> "A Test-bed for Western Drug Companies"
  • Quintiles began operations 1997
  • By 2003 --> dozen+ CROs operating in India
  • Advantages:
  • "Enormous pool" of treatment-naive patients
  • English-speaking, well-trained staff at <1/3 Western wages
  • Only tiny fraction of tested drugs treat tropical diseases common there
  • No guarantee drugs will be affordable in India post-trial
7.3 Ethical Concerns (India)
  • Financial inducements --> paying illiterates more/month than annual salary
  • Illiteracy enables sidestepping informed consent
  • Enrolling patients as if trial = next step in care
  • No guarantee of continued medication post-trial
8. HIV/AIDS Trial Controversies (1997)
  • NIH-funded studies in African countries
  • Tested cheaper alternatives to AZT for preventing mother-to-child transmission
  • Control groups received placebo despite known effective therapy existing
  • Marcia Angell (NEJM) --> "we have not come very far from Tuskegee at all"
  • Debate split bioethics establishment
  • Led to formal reconsideration of ethical standards
9. Implications for Health Disparities
  • Inclusion-and-difference paradigm may reinforce biology-focused understanding of race
  • Risk: detracting attention from sociopolitical causes of health disparities
9.1 Who Benefits?
  • Critical analysis suggests:
  • Paradigm reinforces overemphasis on biology
  • May not actually reduce health disparities
  • But: recruitment imperative remains even for revisionist research programs
9.2 Author's Conclusion
  • Recruitmentology is hybrid --> both social actor and census category conceptions coexist
  • Tension between:
  • Engagement approach: addressing interlocutor whose trust must be gained, history understood
  • Engineering approach: predicting tactics most likely to succeed through formal methods
  • Globalization makes engagement less likely --> increased inequality in power between researcher and subject
10. Key Studies Cited as Success Models
  • African-American Antiplatelet Stroke Prevention Study (AAASPS)
  • Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) Collaborative Research Group
  • Strong Heart Study
  • Women's Health Initiative (WHI)
  • 40 centers total, 10 designated as minority centers
  • Achieved ethnic representation close to US population
11. Methodological Notes
  • Data from 72 semi-structured interviews across multiple US cities
  • Interviews included: NIH/FDA/DHHS officials, clinical researchers, pharma scientists, advocates, bioethicists, Congressional staff
  • Additional sources: agency documents, archival materials, journal articles, media reports
