The Promise and Perils of Industry-Funded Science
  • Source: Philosophy Compass, Vol. 13, 2018 (e12544)
  • Topic: Analysis of industry-funded scientific research - its benefits, risks, and proposed solutions
  • Author(s): Bennett Holman (Yonsei University), Kevin C. Elliott (Michigan State University)
1. Introduction: The Gap in Philosophy of Science
  • Philosophy of science historically focused on physics - Mayr noted biology was ignored in 1969
  • Contemporary philosophy of science focuses on publicly funded research - rarely includes industry case studies (e.g., Dow Chemicals)
  • Industry accounts for ~2/3 of all R&D funding in developed countries (OECD, 2016)
  • Influence expanding to public sector due to changes in university and government policies over past 40 years
  • Scandals drove attention to industry bias: tobacco, acid rain, ozone hole, climate change, chemicals, pharmaceuticals
  • Systematic correlations found between private sector funding and favorable results for funders
2. The Promise of Industry-Funded Research
2.1 Patent System & Business Model Development
  • Bayh-Dole Act (1980s) enabled academic researchers to patent inventions
  • Patent system resolved tensions between science norms (openness) and business demands (profitability)
  • Parke-Davis example: sent employees worldwide to find medicinal plants, published lab reports openly
  • Ethical dilemma: when company didn't patent (following norms), competitors who did undercut their R&D costs
  • Davis wrote: "Our ethical course is leading us astray...we shall be ground to pieces"
2.2 Application Innovation & Design Rules
  • Magnetic sensor technology case (Wilholt, 2006):
  • University researchers discovered resistance fluctuations in conducting layers between ferromagnetic films
  • Commercial applications for digital data storage were clear
  • Physics not well-established when IBM and Philips research labs conducted most work
  • Design rules emerged: locally applicable, empirically derived regularities guiding optimal design
  • Application innovation: important contributions made to theory during commercially driven research
  • Theory improvement needed for cost-effective innovation and products that function without collapsing
2.3 Reproducibility Advantages
  • Standardized protocols followed in many industry labs
  • Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) guidelines include rigorous safeguards for protocol adherence
  • Argument: reproducibility problems in academic research can be alleviated by working with industry (Edwards, 2016)
2.4 Basic Research Contributions
  • Nobel prizes awarded to scientists for work completed in private sector
  • Shuji Nakamura: Blue LED development at Nichia Corporation → Nobel Prize
  • Eric Betzig: super high-resolution fluorescence microscopy at New Millennium Research → Nobel Prize
  • Charles Kao: fiber optics work at Standard Telecommunication Laboratories → Nobel Prize
  • Significant resources of private sector enable innovations in both basic and applied research
3. The Perils of Industry-Funded Research
3.1 Topic Selection Bias
  • Private research criticized for influencing topic choices in questionable ways (Brown, 2008)
  • Example: exercise can alleviate depression but unlikely to receive industry attention because it won't generate profits
  • Gaps in knowledge result from:
  • Failure to investigate particular topics
  • Deliberate efforts to suppress or cast doubt on information threatening financial interests
  • Agnotology: study of how ignorance is created and maintained (Proctor, 2012)
  • Treating ignorance as social product reveals strategies and mechanisms behind it
3.2 Selective Funding & Evidence Manipulation
  • Industrial selection effect (Holman & Bruner, 2017): industry funding systematically biases community even if every scientist is rational and unbiased
  • Case: 1980s cardiac drugs:
  • Hundreds of thousands died from drugs prescribed to prevent cardiac arrests
  • Before FDA approval, legitimate debate existed about methodology
  • Many researchers found drugs unsafe
  • Industry selectively funded researchers using methods favoring their drugs
  • These researchers rose to prominence and built consensus around favorable assessment methods
  • Key insight: views didn't change from funding, but community became biased because of selection effect
3.3 Design Bias & Data Manipulation
  • Data fabrication/falsification occurs but appears relatively rare (Doucet & Sismondo, 2008)
  • Design bias is more common: companies shape studies to generate favorable results
  • Catalogued strategies (Safer, 2002; Smith, 2005):
  • Give competitor's drug in overly high dose → makes their drug look safer by comparison
  • Give competitor's drug in low dose → makes theirs look more effective
  • Selectively report outcomes
  • Present positive subgroups, secondary analyses, or post hoc analyses as if always intended focus
  • Ghostwriting: medical communication companies write papers on behalf of industry sponsors (Elliott, 2010; Healy & Cattell, 2003)
3.4 Rhetorical Manipulation
  • Even accurate data can be presented misleadingly through discussion flexibility (Doucet & Sismondo, 2008; Matheson, 2008)
  • Vioxx case:
  • Early studies showed drug increased cardiovascular events (strokes, heart attacks)
  • FDA warned Merck not to minimize danger in advertising
  • FDA couldn't stop industry-funded academic researchers from calling adverse effects "play of chance"
  • Vioxx ultimately pulled from market after causing >50,000 deaths
3.5 Publication Bias
  • Academic researchers more likely to submit studies with new and interesting results (Song et al., 2010)
  • Important mechanism for compromising epistemic quality of industry-funded research (Chan et al., 2014)
  • Antidepressant case (Turner et al., 2008):
  • Used Freedom of Information Act to get every antidepressant study submitted to FDA (1987-2004)
  • FDA saw: 38 positive studies + 36 negative studies
  • Published literature showed: 48 positive studies + only 3 negative studies
  • Of the 36 negative FDA studies, only 3 published as such (11 others published as positive)
3.6 Skewed Concepts & Categories
  • Eli Lilly fluoxetine (Prozac) example (Brown, 2002):
  • Company wanted to extend patent protection on blockbuster antidepressant
  • Could keep drug under patent longer if found effective for another disorder
  • Pushed psychiatrists to recognize new disorder: premenstrual dysphoric disorder
  • Fluoxetine could treat this new "disorder"
  • Broader pattern: pharmaceutical companies push to expand disease categories to create larger markets (Biddle & Kukla, 2017; Moynihan & Cassels, 2005)
3.7 Social Context Manipulation
  • Philosophers emphasize: cannot understand scientific epistemology without focusing on social interactions among scientists (Longino, 2002; Wilholt, 2009)
  • Environmental arena strategies (Elliott, 2016a):
  • Funding think tanks and front groups spreading preferred messages
  • Attacking opposing messages
  • Funding conferences exploring preferred ideas
  • Creating journals sympathetic to their perspective
  • Tobacco industry pioneered many strategies; replicated by petroleum industry for climate change
3.8 Restricted Access to Data & Materials
  • Clash with Mertonian norms of communal ownership and sharing (Irzık, 2007; Krimsky, 2003)
  • Patent thickets: so many materials/technologies patented that research is hampered (Biddle, 2014a; Sterckx, 2010)
  • Patents supposed to facilitate openness but sometimes restrict research
3.9 Intimidation & Legislative Lobbying
  • Intimidating scientists to prevent spreading threatening information (Elliott, 2016a; McGarity & Wagner, 2008)
  • Legislative lobbying for:
  • Curtailing federal agencies' ability to use and disseminate research
  • Barring independent scientists from peer review panels (without curtailing industry-funded scientists)
  • Redefining "best available evidence" in ways tailored to industry interests (Young, 2017)
3.10 Fostering Sympathetic Scientists
  • Funding research that aligns with company interests
  • Paying scientists to write editorials, articles, and books
  • Creating "key opinion leaders" (KOLs) in educational and advertising campaigns (Elliott, 2010; Moynihan, 2008)
3.11 Public Manipulation Campaigns
  • "Sound science" and "good epidemiology" campaigns to promote industry-preferred messages (McGarity, 2003)
  • Promoting idea that environmental science is "junk science" for failing to meet standards
  • Delaying regulations by claiming ongoing scientific debate (Elliott, 2016a; Michaels, 2008)
3.12 Institutional Corruption
  • Universities less able to serve public interest due to growing entanglement with private sector (Krimsky, 2003)
4. Strategies for Addressing Weaknesses
4.1 Topic Selection Solutions
  • Krimsky's insight: three categories exist - intellectually motivated, profit-motivated, and public interest science
  • Public interest science answers questions the public needs but are neither interesting to scientists nor profitable (often threatening profits)
  • Proposed solutions:
  • Health Impact Fund (Pogge, 2009)
  • Advance market commitments
  • Prizes for innovations serving disadvantaged populations (Elliott, 2017)
  • Limitation: unclear if strategies can be employed widely enough
  • Best approach: generate sufficient public funding to override industry effects (Elliott, 2011; McGarity & Wagner, 2008)
4.2 Data Collection & Analysis Safeguards
  • Good Laboratory Practices (GLP): record-keeping system may prevent fabrication/falsification (Conrad & Becker, 2012)
  • Limitation: GLP inadequate for design bias and rhetorical presentation problems
  • Standardization risks: locking outdated or questionable methods in place (Elliott, 2016b; Wickson & Forsberg, 2015)
  • "Firewalls": create separation between those who design/perform studies and private funders (DeAngelis & Fontanarosa, 2008; Krimsky, 2003)
  • Regulatory agencies: can evaluate interpretation of important studies but limited by time and money
  • Standardized protocols and reporting guidelines: limitations exist (Elliott, 2016b; Moermond et al., 2016)
  • Reporting guidelines effectiveness for rhetorical abuses may be limited (Doucet & Sismondo, 2008)
4.3 Publication Bias Remedies
  • Statistical detection possible for some publication bias cases (Holman, 2018)
  • Trial registries: optimal function is recording all studies begun to make hiding unfavorable results difficult (Goldacre, 2012)
  • Current US registry limitations:
  • Not retroactive → only new studies registered
  • Compliance spotty at best
  • Some trial types not required to register
  • Only requires summary of results, not detailed data (Goldacre, 2013; Goldacre et al., 2016; Prayle et al., 2012)
  • Registries as infrastructure: enable audits of available data → more accurate reliability assessment + pressure on poor performers through naming and shaming (Goldacre, 2015)
  • AllTrials movement: launched "Open Trials" collaborative open database project
  • FDAAA trial tracker: monitors compliance with FDA's trial reporting requirements (DeVito et al., 2018; Goldacre & Gray, 2016)
4.4 Addressing Skewed Concepts
  • Few easy solutions available
  • Call for broader scientific community to challenge questionable new concepts/categorizations (Elliott, 2018)
  • Effectiveness depends on adequate venues for critical review (Longino, 2002; Rolin, 2017)
  • Concern: venues often insufficient to combat commercial pressures (Biddle, 2007; Fernández Pinto, 2014; Jukola, 2015)
  • Even when established: industry has captured such venues to further own aims (Holman & Geislar, 2018)
4.5 Access to Data & Materials
  • University policy changes:
  • Require investigators share data/materials timely even with private funding
  • Pursue nonexclusive licenses for patented innovations
  • Federal patent policy changes:
  • Strengthen research exemptions
  • Lessen probability of "patent thickets" crowding out innovation (Biddle, 2014a; Biddle, 2014b)
  • Medical patent law strengthening: grant patents only to drugs offering significantly greater effectiveness, safety, or convenience (Angell, 2004)
  • Limitation: significant changes to patent system face severe pressure from private sector
4.6 Social Context Manipulation
  • Lawsuits: discourage egregious activities like harassing scientists or disseminating deceptive information (McGarity & Wagner, 2008)
  • Exposing relationships: journalists, lawyers, academics uncovering financial ties between companies and scientists, physicians, think tanks, front groups
  • Limitation: effectiveness somewhat limited
4.7 Combating Public Manipulation & Institutional Corruption
  • Aggressive public funding on sensitive topics creates extensive high-quality research that can overwhelm efforts to skew discourse (Elliott, 2011; Shrader-Frechette, 2007)
  • Extreme option: socialize all such research (Brown, 2017)
5. Recommendations for Future Work
5.1 Limitations of Current Solutions
  • Most solutions not particularly promising - limited potential or very difficult to implement effectively (Elliott, 2018)
  • Disclosure of financial conflicts is most common solution but has very limited effectiveness (de Melo-Martín & Intemann, 2009; Doucet & Sismondo, 2008)
  • Clear need for philosophers to diagnose and address concerns in privately funded research
5.2 Need for Comprehensive Approach
  • Avoid focusing on individual problems without considering full array of concerns
  • Example: GLP alleviates data falsification/fabrication but does nothing for design bias or publication bias (Myers et al., 2009)
  • Recommendation: develop combination of many different solutions
5.3 Science Court Proposal (Biddle, 2013)
  • Structure: controversial policy-relevant questions debated in adversarial format
  • Key features:
  • Industry explicitly recognized as advocates, not dispassionate fact-gatherers
  • Advocacy counter-balanced by team of scientists actively taking contrary position
  • Process presided over by scientists with no intellectual or financial stake in outcome
  • Supplementary strategies needed: maintaining researchers without conflicts, trial registries as epistemic scaffolding
5.4 Arms Race Framework (Holman, 2015)
  • Dynamic: asymmetric arms race between reformers and profit-oriented companies
  • Each party constantly innovating to undermine the other's strategies
  • Three lessons for policy evaluation:
  • Policies must include means to assess when undermined by opponent's countermeasures
  • Policies must be robust to foreseeable responses → pre-implementation assessment trying to think like industry to devise circumvention methods
  • Most durable solutions change underlying incentive structure → as long as epistemic and financial goals conflict, pressure to undermine reform continues
5.5 Focus on Conditions Rather Than Just Solutions
  • Identify conditions where epistemic concerns are most likely to arise (Elliott, 2014)
  • Less concern: when industry funds early-stage research to develop new products (e.g., magnetic sensor technology)
  • More concern: when industry funds research to fight environmental liability or regulations
  • Relatively unproblematic: when easy to assess success/failure (e.g., consumer products that clearly work or malfunction)
  • Nearly impossible to prevent bias: when strong financial incentives to obtain favorable results
  • Philosophers' role in problematic cases: highlight potential problems and explore ways to promote effective critical interaction within broader scientific community
  • Easier cases: fewer incentives for misleading research → easier to develop effective solutions and maximize benefits
5.6 Conclusion
  • Ernst Mayr would be at home at philosophy-of-science conferences today - discipline strengthened by broadened scope
  • Same potential exists for studying industry-funded science
  • Strong need: develop collective understanding of benefits alongside weaknesses
  • Without doing so, critiques remain somewhat provincial
  • Philosophers working at remove: can improve understanding of conditions under which industry produces reliable science → structure institutions to maximize benefits
  • Philosophers engaged publicly: can help public interest groups deconstruct biased science → profound sense of meaning to "doing philosophy"
  • Both types needed: much more work required
