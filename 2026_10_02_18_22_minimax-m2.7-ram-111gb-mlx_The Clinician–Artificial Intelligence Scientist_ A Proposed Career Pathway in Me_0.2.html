The Clinician–Artificial Intelligence Scientist: A Proposed Career Pathway in Medicine
  • Source: Lancet Digital Health 2026
  • Topic: Hybrid career pathway bridging clinical practice and AI development in medicine
  • Author(s): Wu Y, Ong AYC, Chen H, Yang GD, Tham YC, Keane PA, Wong TY
1. Introduction: The Clinician–AI Scientist Role
  • Healthcare professional linking AI technological development with real-world clinical adoption
  • Analogous to traditional clinician–scientist but adapted for AI era
  • Holds frontline clinical experience + continuous patient care responsibilities
  • Equipped to formulate clinically-driven research questions and identify AI solutions for clinical problems
1.1 Why This Role Is Needed
  • Only 0.33% of AI articles (39/11,839) are randomised controlled trials evaluating AI in actual clinical practice
  • Many AI models achieving superhuman benchmark performance fail to produce meaningful clinical benefit
  • Key failure factors:
  • Black-box design --> fosters clinician mistrust
  • Misalignment with clinical workflows
  • Incompatibility with deployment constraints
1.2 The Translation Gap
  • AI development-to-adoption lifecycle requires unique healthcare professionals
  • Must understand both clinical needs AND AI technology fundamentals
  • Central to successful large-scale deployment of medical AI models
2. Real-World Implementation Examples
2.1 Diabetic Retinopathy Screening
  • Problem: Robust AI systems falter during real-world adoption due to:
  • Variation in fundus image quality
  • Added workflow burden for image acquisition
  • Difficulty identifying appropriate patient populations
  • Gaps in referral pathways for patients needing treatment
  • Clinician–AI scientist role:
  • Defines clinically meaningful outcomes and deployment conditions
  • Designs prospective evaluations under real-world constraints
  • Determines where human oversight remains necessary
  • Generates evidence to support, restrict, or delay implementation
  • Leads multidisciplinary governance (clinicians + data engineers + patient representatives)
  • Example: Singapore's national diabetic retinopathy screening programme
2.2 AI-Stethoscope in Primary Care
  • AI-stethoscope allows primary care physicians to detect heart diseases without cardiologist-level auscultatory skills
  • Retrospective studies: performed better than human physicians
  • TRICORDER trial (cluster-randomised): introduction did NOT significantly increase heart-failure detection
  • Gap: Clinical workflow barriers and insufficient understanding of primary care physicians' behaviour
3. How This Role Differs from Existing Disciplines
3.1 Comparison Matrix
Role Focus Clinician–AI Scientist Distinction
Clinicians in AI research Domain knowledge for specific medical questions Not systematically involved in full translational lifecycle; often presented with mature solutions
Clinical informatician Optimising health information systems and workflows Focuses on AI lifecycle, not just data integration
Traditional clinician–scientist Translating fundamental discoveries into medical knowledge Does not typically participate in AI implementation and adoption
Data scientist Technical depth in hospital data acquisition/organisation/use Lacks clinical contextualisation and clinical-facing role
3.2 Unique Value Proposition
  • Not an extension of any single discipline --> distinct professional function at intersection of multiple disciplines
  • AI functions as clinical intervention --> evaluation, implementation, governance are inseparable
4. Distinctive Responsibilities of Clinician–AI Scientist
4.1 End-to-End Lifecycle Oversight
  • Maintains oversight from initial clinical question through:
  • Model development
  • Prospective validation
  • Post-deployment surveillance
4.2 Clinical Leadership for AI Technical Teams
  • Defines meaningful clinical objectives
  • Critiques model behaviour beyond standard performance metrics
4.3 Ensuring Tangible Clinical Outcomes
  • Delivers patient benefit rather than statistical accuracy alone
  • Demonstrates clinical utility and equity
4.4 Required Clinical-Facing Capabilities
  • Defines clinically meaningful outcomes
  • Recognises harmful behaviours and failure modes
  • Determines acceptable clinical trade-offs
5. Role Profiles: Four Subspecialisations
5.1 The Problem Formulator
  • Role: Identifies unmet clinical needs --> translates into soluble technical problems
  • Example:
  • Notices early signs of diseases missed in clinical setting
  • Defines specific targeted population
  • Curates available clinical information
  • Determines exclusion criteria to remove artifacts
  • Specifies clinically relevant sensitivity thresholds
5.2 The Evidence Architect
  • Role: Designs rigorous prospective studies to move algorithms beyond in silico performance
  • Example:
  • Identifies limitations in existing diagnostic AI studies (selection bias, weak comparators)
  • Designs RCTs testing usability and workflow integration
  • Defines endpoints measuring patient outcomes, not just model accuracy
5.3 The Implementation Lead
  • Role: Oversees last mile of integrating validated models into complex hospital systems
  • Example:
  • Works with hospital IT teams and operational leaders
  • Redesigns model interface for workflow integration
  • Ensures AI output appears at right moment in workflow
5.4 The Clinical Champion and Steward
  • Role: Uses AI tools in practice --> provides iterative feedback on efficiency, usability, safety
  • Example:
  • During routine use of automated triage tool, detects patterns of false predictions
  • Documents adverse events and usability frictions
  • Feeds real-world data back to technical team for recalibration

7. Core Competency Domain Details
7.1 Clinical Domain Expertise
  • Mastery of disease pathophysiology, clinical workflows, patient-facing care
  • Provides clinical frame within which AI tools are designed, validated, evaluated
Progression by Seniority
  • Foundation: Applies AI-assisted tools under direct supervision; recognises clinical contexts amenable to AI
  • Development: Formulates clinical problems suitable for AI solutions; interprets EHR, imaging, pathology data
  • Independence: Independently frames clinically valid AI research questions; designs clinical validation studies
  • Leadership: Clinical domain authority within subspecialty; sets standards for AI tool evaluation
Current Training Gaps
  • AI not systematically integrated into core clinical curricula
  • No validated AI competency assessment tools for trainees
  • Clinical educators rarely hold substantive AI expertise
7.2 AI Technical Proficiency
  • Ability to design, develop, validate, critically appraise AI models
  • Understanding of: model architectures, training data requirements, evaluation metrics, common failure modes
Progression by Seniority
  • Foundation: Understands core AI concepts (supervised, unsupervised, deep learning); interprets performance metrics
  • Development: Specifies clinical requirements for AI development; co-leads model evaluation and feasibility studies
  • Independence: Directs technical teams in building clinically grounded, deployment-ready solutions
  • Leadership: Sets institutional standards for clinical AI evaluation; leads quality assurance and accountability frameworks
Current Training Gaps
  • Few clinical training pathways include hands-on AI technical model development
  • Most available content is awareness-level only
  • No standardised AI technical curriculum validated for clinician trainees
7.3 Research Methodology and Critical Appraisal
  • Rigorous application of study design to AI evaluation
  • External validation methodology, trial design, clinically meaningful outcome measures
Progression by Seniority
  • Foundation: Understands basic research design principles; critically appraises published AI studies
  • Development: Designs AI validation and evaluation studies; applies appropriate statistical methods; publishes as first author
  • Independence: Designs and leads clinical AI trials; competes for research grants; establishes methodological norms
  • Leadership: Methodological authority in clinical AI; leads guideline and standards development
Current Training Gaps
  • AI reporting standards not consistently taught in clinical training
  • Limited dedicated clinical-AI research methodology courses
  • AI-specific statistical methods rarely covered in clinical pathways
7.4 Translational Science and Clinical Implementation
  • Capacity to lead full AI development lifecycle: problem framing --> regulatory approval --> deployment --> post-market surveillance --> iterative improvement
Progression by Seniority
  • Foundation: Understands AI development lifecycle and deployment barriers; familiar with regulatory terminology
  • Development: Participates in AI tool piloting and workflow integration; engages with regulatory pathways under supervision
  • Independence: Leads regulatory submissions and implementation strategies; builds clinical-technical research partnerships
  • Leadership: Shapes institutional and national regulatory policy; directs large-scale AI adoption programmes
Current Training Gaps
  • No standardised regulatory training pathway for clinical-AI researchers
  • Few posts combine clinical practice with regulatory engagement
  • Health technology assessment skills absent from most clinical training
7.5 Ethics, Equity, and Governance
  • Recognises and mitigates algorithmic bias
  • Ensures equitable model performance across patient populations
Progression by Seniority
  • Foundation: Understands ethical principles related to AI development; aware of algorithmic bias and health equity implications
  • Development: Conducts bias and fairness assessments; applies fairness metrics; contributes to ethics review processes
  • Independence: Leads bias mitigation programmes; designs equity-centred AI studies and evaluation frameworks
  • Leadership: Leads institutional and national ethics and governance bodies; contributes to international policy standards
Current Training Gaps
  • Ethics and bias assessments not standardised in medical education
  • No board-certified pathway for clinical AI ethics
  • Credentialing frameworks remain nascent and inconsistently applied
8. Challenges in Training and Careers
8.1 Structural Barriers
  • Developing competence across clinical practice, research methodology, AND rapidly evolving AI methods is demanding
  • Pace of AI advancement far exceeds traditional biomedical fields --> persistent pressure to remain current
8.2 Time Constraints
  • Competing clinical service duties reduce time available for research
8.3 Career Infrastructure Gaps
  • No structured training programmes
  • Minimal dedicated funding for translational clinical AI research
  • Small number of established role models
  • No professional bodies with remit to define standards or advocate for this role
8.4 Identity Dilemma
  • Risk of being "not clinical enough" as clinicians
  • Risk of being "not technical enough" as AI engineering collaborators
9. Strategic Pathway for Development
9.1 Individual-Level Strategies
Technical Competencies (Minimum Requirements)
  • Deep understanding of AI system behaviours and limitations
  • Familiarity with AI technical concepts and model evaluation metrics
  • Provides essential vocabulary for effective communication with technical experts
Clinical Research Competencies
  • Study design for AI validation and outcome selection
  • Data analysis and interpretation appropriate for healthcare AI applications
Governance Knowledge
  • Local governance processes and ethical nuances
  • Data privacy concepts for real-world health data
  • Health data poverty and algorithmic bias concepts
Programming (Added Advantage)
  • Not strictly necessary but beneficial
Practical Experience
  • Project-based learning and peer support for shared problem-solving
  • Example: Clinical AI journal club model
9.2 Institutional Support Frameworks
Medical School Level
  • Integrate foundational AI concepts into core curriculum
  • Include critical appraisal skills and ethics training
Postgraduate Training Level
  • Structured training pathways with protected research time
  • Fellowship schemes embedding AI research within clinical training
Professional Organisation Level
  • Define core competencies in clinical data science and AI on national level
  • Provide standardised curriculum
Resource Requirements
  • Access to computational resources
  • Access to datasets
  • Pilot funding for early-career clinician–AI scientists
9.3 Funding and Professional Organisation Support
Protected Research Time
  • Balance with demands of clinical service is essential
Funding Advocacy Actions
  • Work with national funding agencies to create career development awards supporting interdisciplinary research
  • Advocate for clinical AI-related funding programmes
Private Foundation Partnerships
  • Provide transitional support for emerging career pathways
Conference and Publication Initiatives
  • Expand from leading journals to advance clinical translation of science
  • Foster sustained dialogue, visibility, and recognition
9.4 Collaborative Framework Development
Shift Required
  • From client-service model to co-creator model
  • Clinical and technical engineering communities must partner as equals
Partnership Principles
  • Grounding algorithms in clinical reality enhances technical innovation
  • Minimises downstream research waste
Role Recognition
  • Partnership model must recognise complementary expertise of different team members
  • Clinician–AI scientist acts as catalyst ensuring clinical perspective is integrated throughout:
  • Design
  • Development
  • Evaluation
10. Proposed Solutions to Training Gaps
10.1 Existing Support Examples (Illustrative)
Level Type of Support
Foundation Integrated clinical-AI dual-degree and joint academic programmes
Development Structured academic training pathways with protected research time; fellowship schemes embedding AI research within clinical training
Independence Government-funded collaborative platforms bridging AI science and biomedicine; AI literacy modules within health professional education curricula
Leadership National health service fellowship programmes supporting clinician-led AI development; international clinician-scientist schemes
10.2 Identified Training Gaps Summary
  • No standardised career pathway or credentialing framework
  • Inconsistent recognition as distinct specialty across institutions
  • Absence of national workforce planning for clinician–AI scientists
11. Conclusion: Path Forward
11.1 Core Argument
  • AI has potential to radically transform medicine
  • Development, evaluation, adoption, and integration have been slow, fragmented, and disappointing
11.2 Proposed Solution
  • New hybrid clinician–AI scientist role with dedicated career pathway
11.3 Requirements for Success
  • Move beyond current ad hoc, fragmented ecosystem
  • Toward comprehensive, systematic, holistic support system
11.4 Essential Elements Needed
Element Description
Clear career pathways and goals Defined progression stages
Agreed measures of success Metrics for evaluating clinician–AI scientists
Appropriate resources and platforms Computational, data, funding access
Role models and mentors Established practitioners in the field
Institutional recognition Formal acknowledgment of role value
11.5 Future Steps
  • Broader multi-stakeholder consensus needed to:
  • Refine competency categories
  • Define formal competency standards
  • Establish training pathways
  • Set thresholds for practice
11.6 Ultimate Goal
  • Enable transformative potential of medical AI --> tangible improvements in clinical care and patient outcomes
