AI Colonoscopy and Adenoma Detection
  • Source: Gastroenterology Conference Session, Vol. 1
  • Topic: Artificial Intelligence Applications in Colorectal Disease
  • Author(s): Wallace M., Badd M.A., Krishnan E., Barkin A., Ulexi M., Kim K., Espinosa E.
1. Real World Impact of AI Colonoscopy Era on Adenoma Detection
  • Source: Mohammad Ali Badd (Mayo Clinic) / Byte et al. (University of Minnesota)
1.1. Background
  • Colorectal cancer --> leading cause of cancer death in U.S. and worldwide
  • Colonoscopy with polypectomy --> cornerstone of CRC prevention
  • ADR (adenoma detection rate) inversely associated with interval post-colonoscopy CRC
  • 1% absolute increase in ADR --> 3% reduction in interval CRC risk + 5% reduction in mortality (Corley N Engl J Med 2010)
  • Up to 1 in 4 polyps missed at conventional colonoscopy (Zhao Gastroenterology 2019)
  • Significant operator variability in detection
  • CADe systems --> real-time deep learning flags suspected polyps during withdrawal
  • Multiple FDA platform-cleared CADe systems --> widespread U.S. adoption since 2021
  • RCTs and meta-analyses show higher ADR with CADe vs standard colonoscopy
1.2. Study Objective & Hypothesis
  • Hypothesis: AI era (2022–June 2025) --> higher adenoma/advanced adenoma detection + measurable reduction in interval CRC
  • Gap identified: trial-level ADR gain may not translate to clinically meaningful reductions in interval CRC
1.3. Methods
  • Database: TriNetX U.S. Collaborative Network (federated EHR data, 67 healthcare organizations)
  • Cohorts:
  • Pre-AI era: January 2015 – December 2019 (~1,600,000 encounters)
  • AI era: January 2022 – June 2025 (~2,000,000 encounters)
  • Excluded: 2020–2021 (COVID disruption + early adoption transition)
  • Population: Adults aged 45–90 years who underwent colonoscopy
  • Exclusions: CRC diagnosis at or within 1 month of colonoscopy
1.3.1. Outcome Definitions (ICD-10 Codes)
  • Adenoma detection: Any benign colonic neoplasm diagnosis within 30 days of colonoscopy
  • Advanced adenoma detection: Similar methodology, within 30 days of colonoscopy
  • Interval CRC: New diagnosis of malignant colorectal neoplasm from 6–36 months post-index colonoscopy
1.3.2. Important Distinction
  • Traditional ADR --> unit-level quality metric using screening colonoscopies only, cross-referenced with pathology
  • This study used ICD-10 codes for benign colonic neoplasm and advanced adenoma diagnoses
  • Important limitation in granularity of detection data
1.3.3. Statistical Methods
  • 1:1 propensity score matching to balance covariates
  • Matched variables:
  • Demographics: age, sex, race, ethnicity
  • Comorbidities: hypertension, diabetes, CKD, CHF, ischemic heart disease, COPD, dyslipidemia, tobacco/alcohol use
  • Indications: screening, surveillance, iron deficiency anemia, GI bleeding, abdominal pain, weight loss
  • BMI
  • Matching goal: Standardized mean difference <0.1
1.4. Results
  • Post-matching: both cohorts ~1,500,000 each
  • Baseline characteristics well-balanced (SMD <0.1 for all variables)
  • Mean age: ~61 years
  • Female: 54% in both cohorts
  • White race: 71%
1.4.1. Primary Outcomes
Outcome Pre-AI Era AI Era Risk Ratio
Adenoma detection 1.8% 33.6% 1.97
Advanced adenoma detection 0.13% 0.19% 1.48
Interval CRC 0.21% 0.11% 0.53
  • All results --> statistically significant
  • Key finding: more adenomas detected upstream → fewer interval cancers downstream
1.5. Limitations
  • Ecological comparison (not direct AI vs no-AI comparison)
  • Time period includes other quality improvements:
  • ↑ focus on withdrawal time
  • ↑ focus on clean bowel prep
  • ADR feedback programs
  • EHR database limitations:
  • Care delivered outside contributing health system may be missed
  • Both arms equally affected by this limitation
  • Reliance on ICD-10 diagnosis codes rather than direct pathologic review
  • Within AI era, many CADe systems exist with different sensitivities and integration workflows
  • Unmeasured confounders:
  • Endoscopist-level ADR metrics
  • Procedure quality metrics
  • Sedation type
  • Prep adequacy
1.6. Conclusions & Context
  • Findings consistent with CADe trial literature showing higher ADR with CADe
  • Directional improvements across unselected multi-institutional real-world population (vs controlled settings)
  • Most trials looked at adenoma detection but not interval cancer
  • Study demonstrates reduction in interval CRC across AI adoption period
  • Interpretation: improvements likely reflect broader advancement in endoscopy quality, not isolated CADe effect → hypothesis generating
2. AI in Histological Image Diagnosis: EfficientNet B7
  • Source: Elangavan Krishnan team (University of Louisville)
  • Presenter: Gaurav Shankar Palniswami (MUOC Internal Medicine)
2.1. Background
  • ~150k new CRC cases diagnosed in U.S. annually
  • 1.93 million global CRC burden
  • ~900k deaths from CRC worldwide annually
2.1.1. Diagnostic Challenges
  • Diagnostic delay despite screening programs
  • Inter-observer variability: 10–25% between expert pathologists reviewing same slides
  • Access inequity: majority of CRC deaths in low/middle-income countries
  • Sub-Saharan Africa: 1 pathologist per 500,000 people
  • Time-intensive review process
2.1.2. AI Solution Rationale
  • Automate initial extraction from histopathological slides
  • Provide rapid results for pathologist triage
  • Reduce time burden in high-volume networks
  • Bridge access gap (can work with limited internet)
2.2. EfficientNet B7 Architecture
  • Convolutional neural network (deep learning architecture family)
  • Deep layers for understanding image data from histopathology slides
  • 66 million parameters (vs EfficientNet B0: ~5–6M, B3: ~12M)
  • Trade-off: more parameters → better feature extraction but ↑ computational cost
2.2.1. Training Data Preparation
  • Datasets obtained from Cancer Imaging Archive
  • Expert GI pathologists classified images before training
  • Two independent pathologists validated classification
  • Focus on high-quality features:
  • Nucleoli
  • Nuclear ratio
  • Other histologic subtype characteristics
2.3. Methods
  • Dataset split: 60% training / 20% internal validation / 20% external validation
  • Total images: ~10,000
  • External validation conducted via website hosting (simulating real-world deployment)
  • Goal: test if model learns patterns vs memorizes
2.4. Results
  • Binary classification: benign vs malignant (not subtyping/grading)
2.4.1. Internal Validation Performance
  • Accuracy, precision, recall --> >98% across all metrics
2.4.2. External Validation (2,000 images)
  • Confusion matrix results:
  • Minimal misclassifications (~10 false positives, ~9 false negatives)
  • Error rate: 0.87% (10/2,000 images)
  • F1 and F2 scores --> >95% in external validation
  • AUROC approaching 0.99–1.00
  • Previous literature: best results ~0.8–0.9 AUROC
  • Model shows reproducibility across repeated trials
2.4.3. Overfitting Prevention
  • Chose relatively lightweight model (vs deeper architectures)
  • More parameters → more memorization risk
  • External validation via website confirmed generalization
2.5. Clinical Application & Speed
  • Inference time: ~0.6–0.7 seconds per image
  • 1,000 images: ~50–60 seconds total
  • Can work with minimal internet connection (useful in resource-limited settings)
  • Designed as triage tool for pathologists, not standalone diagnostic
2.6. Limitations & Future Directions
  • Current limitation: Binary classification only (benign vs malignant)
  • Does not identify subtype, prognosis, or staging
  • Compute cost: initial training expensive but website deployment makes it free for end-users
  • Validation loss in external testing (expected, being addressed)
  • Future work:
  • Multi-institutional cohort studies
  • MSI instability detection from histopathology alone
  • Genetic marker prediction from images
  • Subtype and staging classification
2.7. Conclusions
  • New benchmark: 0.99 AUROC for colorectal cancer detection from histopathology images
  • Model overcomes overfitting through lightweight architecture choice
  • Potential to accelerate diagnostic pathway significantly
  • Current use case: triage tool (AI flags malignant → pathologist confirms)
  • Can reduce turnaround time from 3–14 days to ~60 seconds for inference
3. Multicenter RCT: CADe Availability in Routine Colonoscopy
  • Source: Alan Barkin (presented results of triple-blinded RCT)
  • Funding: Supported by Canadian Institutes of Health Research
3.1. Background & Rationale
  • CADe colonoscopy evidence shows discrepancies between:
  • Controlled RCTs: ↑ ADR, ↑ adenomas per colonoscopy
  • Pragmatic real-world trials: often no ADR improvement
3.1.1. Meta-Analysis Findings (44 RCTs)
  • ↑ adenomas per colonoscopy with CADe
  • ↑ ADR with CADe
  • No difference in advanced colorectal neoplasia per colonoscopy
  • Small increase only in ACN detection rates
  • Minimal ↑ in procedure time
3.1.2. Real-World Implementation RCTs
  • coloDETECT: Improved detection but operators knew they were in trial
  • NAIAD (UK): ↑ ADR with CADe but returned to baseline when device switched off
  • Australian study: ADR improved only when CADe adopted; lowest-ADR endoscopists did not use it
3.1.3. Possible Explanations for Discrepancies
  • Alert fatigue during procedure
  • False positives reducing endoscopic thrust
  • Hawthorne effect: observed operators behave better than usual
  • Selection bias in RCT populations
3.1.4. Societal Recommendations (Uncertainty)
  • ESGE: 68% voted that "most well-informed patients would favor CADe assistance" (threshold for recommendation: 60%)
  • AGA: No recommendation due to low certainty of evidence
3.2. Study Objective
  • Research question: Does making CADe system available in endoscopy room improve ADR under authentic, unmonitored, routine practice conditions?
3.3. Study Design: Triple-Blinded RCT
  • Fully concealed design:
  • Patients unaware of trial participation
  • Endoscopy staff unaware of trial existence
  • Data analysts blinded to allocation
  • This removes Hawthorne effect from colonoscopy performance
3.3.1. Rationale for Unusual Design
  • CADe is approved by Health Canada, used variably by colonoscopists
  • Budget constraints: not all endoscopy rooms have CADe available
  • Right clinical context for randomization by room availability
3.3.2. Randomization Strategy
  • Arms: Room with CADe vs room without CADe (not CADe used vs not used)
  • Preserves authentic clinical behavior post-CADe implementation
  • 1:1 randomization, stratified by:
  • Center
  • Endoscopist
  • Patient sex
  • Age group
3.4. Methods Details
  • Setting: Three Quebec high-volume academic centers, 2-year period
  • Population: Patients aged 45–89 undergoing screening/surveillance/diagnostic colonoscopy
  • Operators: Board-certified gastroenterologists and general surgeons (full-time)
  • Exclusions: Emergency colonoscopy, known/suspected polyposis, known IBD
3.4.1. Intervention Details
  • System: Medtronic GI Genius CADe system
  • Activated by research staff each morning before team arrival
  • Endoscopists free to deactivate temporarily or for rest of day at own discretion
  • Usage tracked:
  • Center 1: midday + end-of-day assessment
  • Centers 2 & 3: auto-recorded via imaging systems
3.5. Outcomes
  • Primary: Adenoma detection rate (ADR)
  • Secondary:
  • Advanced lesion detection rate
  • Adenomas per colonoscopy
  • Serrated lesion detection (rate and per colonoscopy)
  • Proximal adenoma per colonoscopy
  • Neoplastic polyp rate
  • CADe usage rate
3.6. Statistical Analysis
  • Assumed ADR with CADe: 34%
  • Expected utilization rate: 70% (used for sample size)
  • Assumed ADR without CADe: 25%
  • Absolute risk difference used: 6.3%
  • Required sample: 1,596 patients
  • Primary analysis: Intention-to-treat (ITT)
  • A priori planned per-protocol analysis
3.7. Results
  • CONSORT: 1,744 patients randomized (875 control, 869 intervention)
  • Per-protocol analysis: 1,349 patients
  • Baseline characteristics well-balanced
  • Only non-clinically relevant difference: slightly ↑ case-finding as indication in intervention group
3.7.1. Primary Outcome: ADR
  • ITT analysis: No significant difference between groups
  • Per-protocol analysis: >5% absolute difference (control vs intervention)
  • CADe significantly improved ADR when actually used
3.7.2. CADe Utilization Rate
  • Overall: 57.8% (not even expected 70%)
  • Center 1: ~70% utilization
  • Center 2: <40% utilization
  • Center 3: only ~2% of total enrollment (excluded from center-level analysis)
3.7.3. Subgroup Analyses
  • No difference in sex-stratified ADR
  • Center-level ADR varied (one center had more FIT-positive referrals)
3.7.4. Secondary Outcomes
  • Advanced lesion detection: Significant in BOTH ITT and per-protocol analyses (key finding)
  • All other secondary outcomes: No significant differences between groups
3.8. Limitations
  • 10% crossover rate (addressed by ITT and per-protocol analyses)
  • CADe use tracking challenging but confirmed via multiple methods
  • Generalizability limited to academic centers
  • Only one CADe platform tested (Medtronic GI Genius)
3.9. Conclusions & Implications
  • Triple-blinded concealed RCT is reproducible framework for AI evaluation in GI endoscopy
  • ITT analysis (CADe availability): No significant ADR improvement overall
  • Per-protocol analysis (actual CADe use): Significant ADR improvement
  • Advanced lesion detection: Significantly improved in BOTH ITT and per-protocol
  • Incomplete adoption --> attenuates system-level benefit of CADe technology
  • Clinical AI success/failure depends on workflow integration + clinician adoption (not just algorithm performance)
3.9.1. Policy Implications
  • Equipping rooms with CADe is necessary but not sufficient
  • Tailored implementation strategies required for clinical adoption
  • Must address clinician uptake barriers beyond just equipment availability
4. Upskilling of Optical Diagnosis Performance with CADx Use
  • Source: Megan Ulexi (Montreal University Hospital Center)
  • Duration: 4-year prospective cohort (2021–2025)
4.1. Background
  • CADx (computer-aided diagnosis): AI-based colorectal polyp pathology prediction during colonoscopy
  • Displays real-time classification: neoplastic (adenoma) vs hyperplastic on endoscopy monitor
  • Acts as decision support tool for endoscopist's own optical diagnosis
4.1.1. Uncertainty in AI-Clinician Dynamics
  • Recent perspectives (Lancet, NEJM) highlight gap:
  • Descaling concern: offloaded reasoning → skill deterioration
  • Upskilling potential: AI can sharpen clinician skills within constructive framework
  • CADe literature: 6% decrease in ADR during non-AI-assisted colonoscopies after Caddy withdrawal (hinting at descaling)
  • No equivalent data for CADx despite increasing availability
4.2. Study Objective
  • How does endoscopist optical diagnosis performance evolve over time with vs without CADx use?
4.3. Methods
  • Setting: Montreal University Hospital Center (2021–2025)
  • System: CADx (CatEye system) available throughout study period
  • Endoscopist choice whether to use CADx on each case (not mandated)
  • Optical diagnosis documented systematically for each polyp
  • Histopathology available as reference standard for all polyps
4.3.1. Cohort Characteristics
  • Patients: 1,321 (mean age 65.9 years, 46.2% female)
  • Polyps: 2,855 total
  • CADx-assisted: 1,257 (44%)
  • Unassisted: 1,598 (56%)
  • Mean polyp size: 7.25 mm
  • Histology prevalence:
  • Adenoma: ~60%
  • Hyperplastic: ~20%
  • Serrated lesions: ~6%
4.3.2. Endoscopist Cohort
  • 30 endoscopists (17 trainees)
  • All made at least one unassisted diagnosis
  • 60% made at least one CADx-assisted diagnosis
  • 4 endoscopists performed >75% of their procedures with CADx
4.4. Results: Primary Outcome
  • CADx-assisted accuracy: ↑3.4% per month (statistically significant)
  • Unassisted accuracy: ↑1.1% per month (not statistically significant)
4.4.1. Graphical Trends
  • CADx-assisted (green line): steep positive slope over 4 years
  • Unassisted (blue line): flatter curve, performance plateau around 65–70%
4.5. Results: Secondary Outcomes
  • All diagnostic metrics (except NPV) --> trending higher over time for CADx-assisted cases
  • Significant increases:
  • Specificity: +2.3% per month (CADx-assisted)
  • Positive predictive value: +5% per month (CADx-assisted)
  • Unassisted cases did not show these trends
4.6. Interpretation: Upskilling Mechanism
  • CADx provides immediate feedback during polyp evaluation
  • Reinforces correct pattern recognition through:
  • Independent agreement with endoscopist's diagnosis
  • Directing attention to cases of diagnostic uncertainty
  • Provides objective second opinion in real-time (rare without AI)
  • Without CADx, learning limited by delayed pathology results (~1 month)
4.7. CADe vs CADx: Different Behavioral Effects
  • CADe: Activated during withdrawal, automates detection (passive for endoscopist)
  • CADx: Requires active participation:
  • Switch on system
  • Adequately wash polyp
  • Center in field of view
  • This active engagement may explain different effects on clinician skill development
4.8. Limitations
  • Non-randomized design → significant selection bias risk
  • Endoscopists free to choose CADx use on case-by-case basis
  • Cannot definitively attribute performance changes solely to CADx
4.9. Conclusions & Future Questions
  • Over 4 years, optical diagnosis performance improved faster with CADx use
  • Endoscopists eventually surpassed mean unassisted performance
  • Findings support AI-enhanced learning hypothesis
  • Future research needed: What happens to diagnostic performance if CADx withdrawn after extended use?
5. Multimodal Large Language Models for Polyp Analysis
  • Source: Kyung Kim (Seoul National University Hospital, Korea)
5.1. Background
  • Multimodal LLMs (MLLMs) have shown impressive performance in:
  • Radiology image understanding
  • Histopathology analysis
  • Automated report generation
  • Clinical decision support
  • Applications in gastroenterology/colonoscopy limited and complicated
5.2. Study Objective
  • Investigate MLLM effectiveness for colonoscopic polyp analysis using two adaptation strategies:
  • In-context learning with exemplary images
  • Domain-specific fine-tuning
  • Evaluate potential for polyp detection and localization
5.3. Methods
  • Datasets: Real Colon dataset + Sun dataset (annotated)
  • Two experimental tasks:
  • Task 1: Polyp detection
  • Task 2: Polyp localization (using bounding boxes)
5.3.1. In-Context Learning
  • Model: Gemini 2.5 Pro (commercial)
  • Varied number of example images: 0–128 shots from Sun dataset
  • Evaluation on Real Colon dataset
5.3.2. Fine-Tuning Approach
  • Models: LLaMA 3.2 Vision + MedLLaMA (open-source)
  • Training: >24,000 annotated images from Sun dataset
  • Evaluated on both tasks
5.3.3. Comparison with Human Doctors
  • Three internal medicine residents
  • Three gastroenterologists
  • Same test sets as models
5.3.4. Task Descriptions
  • Polyp detection: "Is there a polyp? If yes, where is it?"
  • Bounding box estimation: Center coordinates + width/height prediction
  • Assesses model's understanding of polyp boundaries independent of detection ability
5.3.5. Evaluation Metrics
  • Detection: Accuracy, precision (detected center location), F1 score
  • Localization:
  • Mean Absolute Error (MAE): average error between predicted and actual values
  • Intersection over Union (IOU): overlap between predicted and actual bounding box
  • Accuracy at IOU ≥0.5
5.4. Results: In-Context Learning
  • Detection accuracy improved with more example shots:
  • Zero-shot: 80%
  • 128 shots: nearly 90%
  • Key finding: Only 8 shots sufficient for meaningful improvement
  • F1 score and accuracy both improved with more examples
  • Performance approached internal medicine resident level
5.5. Results: Fine-Tuned Models
  • After fine-tuning to colonoscopy domain:
  • Polyp detection accuracy: 58% → 93%
  • Localization MAE: ~14% → 3% (↓ error)
  • Accuracy at IOU ≥0.5: 39% → 96%
5.5.1. Comparison with Human Doctors
  • Gastroenterologists still showed higher accuracy than both:
  • Internal medicine residents
  • Gemini (with in-context learning)
  • However, difference between gastroenterologists and Gemini was minimal
  • MLLM showed higher consistency across repeated trials than humans
  • This consistency valuable for clinical quality control
5.6. Clinical Implications
  • In-context learning: Practical without large-scale custom training
  • Few examples (8 shots) enough for meaningful improvement
  • Fine-tuning: Highest accuracy, comparable to expert level
  • Greater consistency than human endoscopists
  • Potential applications:
  • Quality assurance in clinical endoscopy
  • Training support where expert availability limited
5.7. Limitations & Future Directions
  • Current studies on static polyp images (not video)
  • More complex real-time scenarios being developed
  • Future work: 3D polyp perception and size estimation
5.8. Conclusions
  • Both prompting (in-context) and fine-tuning strategies achieve clinically meaningful performance
  • In-context learning: easy to apply, requires minimal examples → practical option without custom training
  • Fine-tuning provides highest accuracy, expert-level performance
  • MLLMs can serve as effective quality assurance and educational tools in clinical endoscopy settings
6. Machine Learning for Polyp Risk Stratification on VA Health Records
  • Source: Ethan Espinosa (VA Boston Health Care System)
6.1. Background
  • Colorectal neoplastic polyps are known precursors of colorectal cancer
  • Current guidelines base risk stratification primarily on colonoscopy findings
  • Electronic health records (EHR) contain comprehensive patient history:
  • Blood panels
  • Clinical history
  • Pathology reports
6.2. Study Objectives
  • Primary: Use ML models integrating EHR data to stratify risk of subsequent neoplastic polyp development
  • Secondary: Use feature importance approaches to identify which clinical features influence neoplasia development
6.3. Data Source & Population
  • Database: VA Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW) - national repository of VHA clinical/administrative data
  • Study period: January 2010 – January 2025
  • Inclusion criteria: Patients with ≥2 colonoscopies ≥8 months apart
6.4. Features Extracted (33 total)
  • Personal data: Smoking history, alcohol use, family CRC history, demographics
  • Lab data: Blood panel values (triglycerides, mean platelet volume, etc.)
  • Pathology data: Raw pathology text from first colonoscopy
6.5. NLP Processing: BioBERT
  • Challenge: Pathology reports are full text → cannot be processed by traditional ML models
  • Solution: BioBERT (NLP transformer-based model for biomedical text mining)
  • Trained classifiers to extract:
  • Neoplastic polyp presence (yes/no) → from words like "neoplasia," "dysplasia"
  • Number of neoplastic polyps (count)
  • Location: Proximal, rectosigmoid, or pan-colonic (both regions)
  • Subtype/grade: Tubular adenoma → adenocarcinoma
6.6. Data Filtering & Cohort Selection
  • Initial patients from CDW: ~408,000
  • Exclusions:
  • IBD diagnosis codes in pathology text
  • Poor bowel preparation (first or second procedure)
  • Outliers (IQR and histogram filtering)
  • Excessive missing information
  • Final study cohort: ~32,000 patients
6.7. Model Development
  • Five models tested:
  • Logistic regression
  • XGBoost
  • Decision tree
  • LightGBM
  • Random forest
6.7.1. Data Splitting
  • Training set: 25,000 patients
  • Test set: 6,000 patients (unseen data)
  • 5-fold cross-validation with minimal variance (~0.01) across folds
6.8. Results: Model Performance
  • Test set ROC performance:
  • All models: 0.71–0.72 (consistent across models)
  • Better than chance (dotted line baseline)
  • Minimal variance in 5-fold cross-validation → supports generalizability
6.9. Feature Importance: SHAP Analysis
  • SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations): Explains ML model predictions
  • Each feature has calculated SHAP value (direction + magnitude of impact)
6.9.1. Top 10 Features by Importance
  • Pathology findings ranked as most important features
  • Four of top 10 features from pathology reports:
  • Number of neoplastic polyps (jars)
  • Presence of neoplastic polyp (grade)
  • Proximal location
  • Pan-colonic involvement
6.9.2. Remaining Features (Personal + Lab)
  • Six of top 10 from personal history and lab values
  • Cumulative impact on prediction still significant
6.9.3. Directionality Examples (SHAP bee swarm plot)
  • Number of polyps: Higher count → positive SHAP value toward neoplasia prediction
  • Zero neoplastic polyps: negative SHAP (lower impact)
  • ≥2 polyps: positive SHAP (higher impact toward prediction)
  • Age: ~60 years threshold for model influence
  • Younger → lower impact on neoplasia prediction
  • Triglycerides: Normal range (~100 mg/dL) → increased influence toward neoplasia prediction
6.10. Clinical Application
  • Model can risk stratify follow-up colonoscopy timing
  • Example: 7–10 year interval guidelines → model helps determine if patient should return earlier (7 years) or later (10 years)
  • Contextualizes patient's individual risk using comprehensive history
6.11. Limitations
  • 90% data exclusion (408,000 → 32,000) due to:
  • Missing information
  • Outlier values
  • Potential for selection bias in included population
  • However, strict filtering ensured all patients had complete data across features
6.11.1. Addressing Bias Concern
  • Changing strictness of exclusion criteria did not significantly affect model performance
  • Trade-off between accuracy and broader population inclusion
6.12. Conclusions
  • Traditional ML models can risk stratify neoplastic polyp development in follow-up procedures
  • Uses lab, clinical, and pathology features from first colonoscopy
  • Pathology reports are major driving factors in model's prediction
  • Accumulation of lab and clinical information has quantifiable influence beyond pathology alone
  • Clinical impact: Helps physicians determine appropriate follow-up colonoscopy timing
