AI Meets the Esophagus: Transforming Detection and Decision-Making
  • Source: QuickShots Session, Digestive Disease Week (DDW)
  • Topic: Artificial intelligence applications in esophageal diagnostics and surveillance
  • Author(s): Nassim Parsa (moderator), Kaplan Leggett (co-moderator), Rick Stefan Eich, Sriharsha Bopana, Ashwin Roe, Chanel Khadipili, Alexander Bartram, Noor Shaker, Carlos Nima, Venkata Sunkisula, Hannah Phillips, Jeremy Massini
1. Early Barrett's Neoplasia Detection (CADe/CATe Systems)
  • Focus --> early subtle lesions vs. colorectal polyps
  • Ground truth = expert endoscopic assessment of visible lesions warranting targeted biopsy
1.1 Study Design
  • Prospective multicenter study (3 Dutch expert centers)
  • 200 Barrett's patients included
  • Expert endoscopists assessed all segments stepwise → ground truth
  • CATe system applied in same manner --> registered detections
1.2 Results
  • 82 patients had visible lesions (ground truth positive)
  • 73/82 detected by CATe → sensitivity = 89%
  • 9 lesions missed by CATe
  • 118 patients had no visible lesion (ground truth negative)
  • 74 false positive detections in 58 of these patients
  • False positive rate --> 0.6 per patient
  • Clinical impact → 1 additional biopsy every 2 patients
  • Average random biopsies per patient = 16
  • Of detected lesions: majority neoplastic (high grade dysplasia or cancer)
  • ~1/3 of detected lesions were non-neoplastic → still true positive endoscopic detections
1.3 Key Insight: Histology vs. Endoscopic Endpoints
  • Traditional endpoint = histology-proven neoplasia
  • Low prevalence in community settings → requires extremely large sample sizes
  • Biopsy sampling may be inadequate or miss lesion entirely
  • Inter-observer variability among pathologists persists
  • Recommendation: Shift to endoscopic endpoints (visible lesion detection)
  • Rationale: aligns with primary purpose of CADe/CATe systems
  • Example: large Chinese RCT (~30,000 patients) showed only minimal AI benefit when evaluated against histology
1.4 Lesions Missed by CATe vs. Detected
  • Missed lesions = flat, small, located near gastric folds
  • Subtle features → expert-level detection still challenging for AI
  • CATe detected these subtle lesions in 2/3 of cases → true positive
1.5 Clinical Implication
  • Primary endpoint should be endoscopic detection of visually suspicious lesions
  • Not histological diagnosis alone
  • This approach is more feasible for low-prevalence diseases
2. Machine Learning for Variceal Hemorrhage Outcomes
  • MELD score limitations: ignores hemodynamics and endoscopy
  • CHIPS score limitations: lacks precision, is categorical
  • Rockall/Glasgow limitations: pre-endoscopic triage, ignores cirrhosis/endoscopy data
2.1 Study Design
  • Retrospective cohort using MIMIC-IV dataset (Beth Israel, 2008–2022)
  • ICD code diagnosis for suspected upper GI bleeding
  • AVH confirmed by EGD text (esophageal variceal banding or cryotherapy injection)
  • Exclusion: prior liver transplant, non-variceal bleeding
2.2 Predictor Variables
  • Demographics and comorbidities: AIDS, sex, cirrhosis etiology, HCC, diabetes, CKD
  • First 24-hour clinical data: BP, HR, MAP, labs (hemoglobin, INR, creatinine, transaminases)
  • Transfusion requirements
  • Vasoactive medication use
  • Endoscopic NLP features: size, location, active spurting, red wale sign, portal hypertension gastropathy grading
2.3 NLP Processing Pipeline
  • EGD reports linked to subject ID and hospitalization cost
  • Extracted: variceal size, location, active spurting, red wale sign, portal gastropathy severity
  • Natural language processing to capture endoscopic free text
2.4 Model Performance (XGBoost)
  • 6-week mortality prediction:
  • XGBoost AUROC = 0.89
  • AUPRC = 0.68 (excellent discrimination)
  • MELD-Na = 0.78
  • CHIPS, Rockall ≈ 0.7
  • 6-week rebleeding prediction:
  • XGBoost AUROC = 0.80
  • MELD-Na = 0.7
2.5 Top Predictors of Mortality (SHAP Analysis)
  • End-organ hypoperfusion with lactate
  • Creatinine → hepatorenal syndrome/AKI
  • Hemodynamic instability (SBP)
  • Active spurting on EGD
  • Esophageal varices finding
  • Early transfusion requirement
  • Vasoactive drug use
2.6 Clinical Decision Pathway
  • Model integrates physiological, anatomical, and clinical treatment data
  • Low risk → standard follow-up
  • Immediate risk → hepatology consult, early TIPS consideration
  • High risk → ICU consideration, early TIPS, prophylaxis
2.7 Limitations and Future Directions
  • External validation needed
  • ICD code misclassification in model input
  • MIMIC dataset lacks pre-admission outpatient data
  • Prospective clinical trial required
3. AI for Supra-Gastric Belching Detection
  • Supra-gastric belching = air brought rapidly into esophagus via diaphragmatic contraction → expelled retrograde before reaching stomach
  • Episodic events, sporadic (few dozen per 24 hours)
  • Events occur in tenths of a second → manual identification is tedious
3.1 Study Design
  • Clinically applicable cohort (not man-made construct)
  • GERD patients with symptoms → 15 test individuals
  • Export PH impedance data to CSV (~90,000 lines per study)
  • Remove meal times → 2-second windows
  • Initial filter: ≥500 ohm rise above baseline
3.2 Machine Learning Algorithm
  • Neural network architecture for event classification
  • Training on all events → predict true vs. false
  • Predictions made on filtered windows
  • 15 test studies with randomized manual marking by blinded assessor
3.3 Results
  • Sensitivity ≈80% overall cohort
  • Sensitivity ↑ significantly when excluding cases with 0–1 events (where chance of detection is low)
  • Mean false positives = 2 per study
  • Highest single-study false positives = 8
  • Machine identified events missed by humans → added clinical value
  • Normal threshold established: >13 supra-gastric belching events = excessive
3.4 Clinical Validation
  • All individuals with normal manual counts → also classified as normal by ML
  • All individuals with excessive manual counts → also classified as excessive by ML
  • Cases near the 13-event threshold: human identified 11, machine identified 13 → close agreement
  • One case: human =14, machine =14 → perfect match even near threshold
3.5 Key Advantages
  • Manual review takes ~30 minutes per study (frame by frame)
  • ML algorithm completed analysis in seconds
  • Minimal false positives → clinically manageable burden
  • No crossover misclassification even without manual review
4. LLMs for Barrett's Esophagus Surveillance Guidelines
  • Problem: adherence to BE surveillance guidelines is suboptimal
  • Common errors: surveillance without clear indication, over-surveillance of confirmed nondysplastic BE, infrequent tailoring based on segment length
4.1 System Architecture
  • Extracts surveillance-relevant BE variables from endoscopy and pathology reports
  • NLP models detect formatting, section headers, specimen identification, diagnoses (BE vs. no BE vs. adenocarcinoma)
  • Identifies dysplasia grade and segment length
  • Rules-based engine assigns guideline-concordant intervals
4.2 Design Rationale
  • Transparent benchmarking and updates as models improve over time
  • Rules-based backend → reduces hallucination risk in generative AI
  • Goal: mitigate generative errors for clinical decision support
4.3 Internal Validation Results
  • Excellent per-variable extraction performance vs. gold standard
  • Great concordance with guidelines for assigning intervals in eligible BE cases
  • Personalized tailoring based on segment length (even when inferred from max length reported)
  • Longitudinal trajectory tracking over time for patients undergoing endoscopic eradication therapy
4.4 External Validation
  • Multicenter veterans cohort (pathology data only)
  • Performance maintained in external dataset
  • Advantage over regex/NLP: captures long tail of edge cases (e.g., "suggestive of intestinalized mucin")
  • No tailoring by segment length or indication (pathology-only data)
4.5 Future Directions
  • Integration with EHR through FHIR interface
  • Testing for trust, usability, and effectiveness in clinical settings
5. AI Models for GERD Treatment Efficacy
  • GERD management remains challenging
  • Patients with different symptoms show variable responses to standard acid suppression
  • Current prediction tools are limited
5.1 Study Design
  • Multi-center real-world study (Guangdong, China)
  • Binary classifiers for therapeutic efficacy prediction
  • Comprehensive clinical data + 3 objective tests (esophageal functional testing)
  • Built by 5 AI models
  • Medication Richmond optimization via regression models using random forest
5.2 Efficacy Prediction Models
  • 153 parameters used in full model with internal 5-fold cross-validation
  • XGBoost showed best performance
  • Average accuracy = 0.65–0.70
  • Internal validation (678 patients, primary center): AUC = 0.67
  • External validation (114 patients, other centers): AUC = 0.63
5.3 Simplified Models (10 Most Important Parameters)
  • Model with manometry or reflux monitoring parameters: accuracy ≈ 70%
  • Model without esophageal functional test results (symptom-based): accuracy ≈ 70%
5.4 Medication Optimization
  • Random forest regression model for regimen optimization
  • Mean absolute error = 3.9 points on 40-point symptom scale
  • Distribution of optimal regimens:
  • Acid suppressant monotherapy = largest proportion
  • Second most common: acid suppressant + prokinetic agents
5.5 Conclusions
  • XGBoost effectively predicts acid suppression efficacy in GERD patients
  • Simplified models maintain good predictive value without functional test results
  • AI can recommend optimal medication regimens
6. Deep Learning for Barrett's Progression Risk
  • Current guidelines: endoscopic surveillance of nondysplastic BE every 3–5 years
  • Limitations: dysplasia can be scattered/subtle, histologic uncertainty, missed dysplasia, non-compliance, expensive strategy
6.1 Model Architecture
  • Weakly supervised multiple instance learning (MIL) model
  • Predicts progression risk based on pre-progression slides
  • MIL allows meaningful interpretation when not all data points impact outcome
  • Not every pixel on slide contributes → model identifies relevant patches
6.2 Study Cohorts (Mayo Clinic)
  • Patients with nondysplastic BE, indefinite for dysplasia, or low grade dysplasia
  • Expert pathologist confirmation of initial diagnosis
  • Progressors: progressed to high-grade dysplasia or cancer ≥12 months after initial BE diagnosis
  • Non-progressors: did not progress with ≥5 years endoscopic follow-up
6.3 Baseline Characteristics
  • Representative of typical BE population: older, overweight men
6.4 Workflow (Computer Vision Automated)
  • Tissue detection → 256×256 pixel patches
  • ResNet model for feature extraction
  • MIL model (adapted from Atramil) for inference
  • Output: percentage probability of progression
6.5 Results
  • Validation set: AUC = 0.86
  • Test set:
  • AUC = 0.87
  • F1 score = 0.84
  • Sensitivity = 0.90
  • Specificity = 1.00
  • Precision = 0.91
6.6 Decision Driving Attention Map
  • ~13,000 patches analyzed per slide
  • Model used ~149 patches to make progression vs. non-progression decision
6.7 Conclusions and Next Steps
  • Weakly supervised deep learning model shows promising performance for BE progression risk prediction
  • External validation ongoing in prospective manner
7. Blood-Based Biomarkers for Esophageal Cancer
  • Focus on plasma proteome using Olink proximity extension assay technology
  • Quantifies ~3,000 proteins per sample
7.1 UK Biobank Proteomic Cohort (Initial Model)
  • ~50,000 healthy UK patients
  • Blood draw at baseline → Olink technology
  • Follow-up over many years → recorded disease development including esophageal cancer
  • Machine learning approach: Cox proportional hazards model
  • Training: 50 random seeds, 50/50 split of cases and controls (3 controls per case)
  • Consensus proteomic models developed from frequency of top proteins across 50 seeds
7.2 Results
  • Blood draw taken 5–6 years before clinical diagnosis → still achieved AUC ≈ 0.90
  • Even with 4-protein model + clinical covariates (highly parsimonious) → AUC ≈ 0.90
  • Key driver protein: REG4 (most frequently included across 50 models)
7.3 Independent Validation Cohort
  • Symptomatic reflux patients (171 samples)
  • Same-day blood draw and endoscopy → accurately annotated plasma
  • Targeted Olink panel including REG4 (top protein from UK Biobank model)
  • Progressive rise in REG4 relative concentration as disease progresses
  • Significant trend testing across disease progression spectrum
7.4 REG4 Biological Validation
  • Visium transcriptomic data: REG4 signal in transcriptome
  • Immunohistochemistry confirms protein expression
7.5 Classification Performance by Stage
  • All cancer (vs. controls): AUC = 0.88
  • T1B and beyond: AUC = 0.96
  • High-risk patients (dysplasia + cancer vs. symptomatic reflux + nondysplastic BE): AUC ≈ 0.80
  • REG4 alone (single protein) achieves this performance
7.6 Conclusions
  • Plasma proteins can identify cancer years before clinical diagnosis
  • REG4 signal progressively increases with disease severity
  • Even single-protein models show clinically useful discrimination
8. Machine Learning for Barrett's Dysplasia Grading
  • BE diagnosis: ≥1 cm columnar-lined epithelium replacing squamous esophagus with intestinal metaplasia on biopsy
  • Dysplasia is most clinically significant finding for neoplastic potential
  • 55% of cases reclassified by expert pathologist (meta-analysis)
  • Inter-observer variability especially problematic for indefinite and low-grade dysplasia
8.1 Study Cohort
  • 140 patients with associated H&E stained whole slide images (low grade + high grade dysplasia)
  • Digitized at 40× magnification → access to nuclear and tissue architecture detail
8.2 Model Architecture
  • Two types of ML models trained:
  • Unsupervised/self-supervised: trained without labels, learns patterns by replicating input
  • Embeddings projected to 2D space → pathologist confirmed meaningful clusters (intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, submucosal gland)
  • Supervised: trained on labeled images using embeddings from unsupervised model
  • Differentiates low grade vs. high grade dysplasia
8.3 Training Process
  • Patient-level images → unsupervised model → patient embeddings
  • Embeddings fed into supervised learning model for dysplasia classification
  • Iterative training until accurate classification achieved
8.4 Results
  • 90% accuracy for classifying low grade vs. high grade dysplasia (full training set)
  • Test set (~30 images from completely unseen data): 90% accuracy
  • Model accurately classified both low and high grade cases
8.5 Future Directions
  • Multi-cohort expansion (currently single hospital)
  • Integration of 53 biomarkers from same cohort for low-grade cases
  • Progression model development: predict which patients progress from LGD/indefinite to HGD within 3–5 years
9. AI and Impedance for Motility Disorders
  • Dysphagia affects 1 in 6 people in the US
  • When barium swallow and EGD are normal → high-resolution manometry is gold standard
  • Chicago Classification: based on pressure metrics of LES and esophagus only (excludes initial inhibition phase)
  • 30–50% of high-resolution manometry for dysphagia → normal (functional dysphagia)
9.1 Key Insight
  • Pressure readings look nearly identical between functional dysphagia patients and normal controls
  • Impedance recordings reveal completely different patterns:
  • Normal: smooth, unobstructed flow
  • Functional dysphagia: obstructed pattern resembling an island
9.2 Study Design (UCSD Health)
  • HRM from UCSD database + normal controls
  • 35 examples per group (7 Chicago Classification subgroups):
  • Achalasia type 2, achalasia type 3
  • Ineffective motility
  • Hypercontractile esophagus
  • EGJ outflow obstruction
  • Functional dysphagia
  • Normal controls
  • 5 swallows per patient → 175 swallows per group = 1,225 total
9.3 Region of Interest
  • 1 second prior to UES relaxation → end of contraction wave
  • Excludes LES (used in Chicago Classification) → tests whether impedance alone can diagnose
9.4 Five CNN Models Tested
  • Pressure data alone
  • Impedance data alone
  • Pressure → then impedance (sequential)
  • Impedance → then pressure (sequential)
  • Two-branch model: two CNNs trained separately on pressure and impedance, merged at later stage
9.5 Implementation
  • TensorFlow in Python
  • Adam optimizer
  • 100-fold Monte Carlo cross-validation
  • Training/validation/testing = 70/15/15 split
  • Patient-level predictions via majority voting
9.6 Multiclass Results (7 Motility Groups)
  • Pressure alone: recall ≈50% across all groups
  • Impedance and two-branch models performed nearly identically to pressure alone in most groups
  • Two-branch model statistically significantly better than pressure-only for functional dysphagia and normal groups specifically
  • Fewer misclassifications in two-branch model vs. pressure-only for functional dysphagia and normals
9.7 Binary Classification Results (Functional Dysphasia vs. Normal)
  • Pressure model: moderate performance
  • Impedance-only model: statistically significantly greater AUC, accuracy, and specificity vs. pressure alone
  • All models incorporating impedance + pressure outperformed pressure-only model
9.8 Key Conclusions
  • Distinct detectable pattern of impedance transit in functional dysphagia vs. normal controls
  • Esophageal patterns alone (excluding LES) sufficient for neural network diagnosis of motility disorders
  • First study to incorporate impedance data from HRIM to train ML model
  • Impedance can reduce false negatives in clinical practice
10. Deep Learning for Esophagitis Grading and Polyp Detection
  • GERD affects 20% of US adults
  • LA classification is global standard for severity grading
  • Problem: substantial inter/intra-observer variability + high-volume endoscopy misses incidental findings
10.1 Study Objectives
  • Grade reflux esophagitis on LA scale (A–D)
  • Simultaneously detect polyps in real time
  • Compare 6 CNN models
10.2 Dataset and Augmentation
  • Publicly available dataset: ~25,000 highly curated images
  • Graded LA A to D + polyps + normal mucosa
  • Augmented to 15× original size for class balance and robustness
10.3 Data Split
  • 70/15/15 train/validation/test split
  • Class balance preserved across splits
10.4 Six Models Compared
Model Accuracy
MobileNet 97–99%
ResNet 97–99%
Inception 97–99%
EfficientNet 99% (best)
ViT (Vision Transformer) 97–99%
ConVMixer 97–99%
10.5 Key Findings
  • EfficientNet outperformed all models with 99% accuracy
  • All models in 97–99% range
  • Errors mirrored human inter-observer variability (LA A vs. B most confused)
  • GradCAM confirmed model focuses on mucosal pathology
10.6 Limitations
  • Single institution dataset (25,000 images = small for deep learning)
  • No external validation
  • Retrospective still images only (no prospective video data)
  • Class imbalance: C and D grades rare vs. A and B
10.7 Future Directions
  • External validation with multicenter/multi-dataset data
  • Prospective video integration for real-world training
  • Endoscopist-in-the-loop approach for real-time agreement testing
  • Expand label set to include Barrett's, eosinophilic esophagitis, and vascular lesions
11. Untethered Capsule for Post-Treatment Barrett's Surveillance
  • After endoscopic eradication therapy (EET) → lifelong surveillance recommended
  • High-grade dysplasia/intramucosal carcinoma: annual endoscopy after initial 3 and 6-month follow-ups
  • Cost, time, and effort burden for patients
11.1 Tethered Capsule Endomicroscopy (TCE)
  • Optical coherence tomography (OCT) device at end of capsule
  • Size: ~11 mm (medium pill-sized)
  • Creates 3D cross-sectional images of esophagus
  • Resolution: 7 μm over 5 mm depth
  • Point-of-care in clinic → no sedation required
  • Patient swallows capsule → images captured on way down AND way up
11.2 TCE Image Features
  • Normal epithelium: dark layer (epithelium) → smooth surface, normal esophageal wall layers visible
  • Nondysplastic BE: irregular epithelial surface (bumpy), loss of normal wall layer architecture
  • Dysplastic BE: atypical glands with debris, irregular surface + lack of surface maturation (darker epithelial layer with shadowing)
11.3 Acceptability Study Results
  • 35 patients with proven CRIM (complete remission of intestinal metaplasia) after EET
  • 91% somewhat to extremely preferred TCE over endoscopy
  • Patients likely to undergo TCE again and as future alternative
  • TCE detected both recurrences found on endoscopy (1 nondysplastic BE + 1 intramucosal adenocarcinoma)
11.4 One-Year Surveillance Study
  • Same 35 patients → 24 returned for second TCE before one-year surveillance endoscopy
  • Expert in TCE image interpretation rated each study (blinded to endoscopy and histopathology)
  • Ground truth = histopathology results on surveillance biopsies
11.5 Performance Results
  • 1 patient had endoscopic recurrence of nondysplastic BE (true positive)
  • 4 patients rated as having recurrent BE on TCE
  • TCE detected the 1 true recurrence (100% sensitivity)
  • 3 false positives: patients rated as BE on TCE but no BE on endoscopy/biopsy
  • These 3 false positives were also false positives in acceptability study → consistent over time
11.6 Performance Metrics
  • Sensitivity = 100%
  • Negative predictive value = 100%
  • Specificity = 80%
  • Accuracy = 80%
11.7 Key Insight
  • False positivity is consistent over time → imaging features remain stable
  • Next step: direct image-to-histology comparison to determine what features cause false positives (post-treatment changes vs. true recurrence)
12. Esophageal Mucosal Impedance for GERD in Bariatric Patients
  • 20–30% of bariatric surgery patients develop de novo reflux after sleeve gastrectomy
  • Preoperative GERD is main predictor of postoperative GERD
  • Current tools inadequate: symptoms alone insufficient, normal endoscopy cannot rule out GERD, pH testing not scalable
12.1 Rationale
  • Acid aggression → dilatation of intercellular spaces in esophageal mucosa
  • Dilated spaces change mucosal impedance
  • Device: MiVue (point-of-care mucosal impedance during regular endoscopy)
  • Endocap adapted to scope tip
  • Touch mucosa → measures impedance
  • Report shows values from distal to proximal esophagus
12.2 GERD Pattern on Mucosal Impedance
  • Low impedance at GE junction → gradually normalizes toward proximal esophagus
  • Normal: steady high impedance across entire esophagus
12.3 Study Design (Prospective)
  • All patients undergoing bariatric surgery assessment
  • Routine GERD screening: EGD + PPI use + GERD questionnaire + esophageal mucosal impedance
  • pH monitoring ordered at surgeon's discretion (only 26% had Bravo)
  • Detailed clinical, demographic, and endoscopic data collected
12.4 Patient Characteristics (101 patients enrolled)
  • 84% female
  • Average BMI = 48 kg/m²
  • 50% African American
  • No complications with MiVue device → safe
12.5 GERD Prevalence
  • 35% classified as GERD by abnormal mucosal impedance
  • No difference in clinical characteristics between normal and abnormal groups
12.6 Mucosal Impedance Patterns
  • Normal group: steady high impedance across esophagus (GE junction to proximal)
  • GERD/abnormal group: low at GE junction → gradual normalization proximally
  • Stratification by esophagitis grade:
  • Grade C: classic GERD pattern (very low → high)
  • Grades A and B: large overlap between normal/abnormal
12.7 GERD Symptoms vs. Objective Findings
  • Abnormal impedance group → significantly higher GERD symptom scores
  • Severity of esophagitis: only grade C found in abnormal group; grade B split 50/50
  • DeMeester score significantly higher in abnormal impedance group (some overlap)
  • Percent time pH <4: not significantly different (likely Type II error → more data needed)
12.8 Key Finding: Silent GERD
  • 51 patients with no GERD symptoms and only grade A esophagitis or less → easily classified as "no GERD" for surgical planning
  • Of these: 21% had abnormal mucosal impedance consistent with GERD
  • Those who had pH testing (surgeon's discretion) → positive pH test = true non-erosive reflux disease
12.9 Conclusions
  • Objective GERD diagnosis is necessary before bariatric surgery (endoscopy + symptoms insufficient)
  • Esophageal mucosal impedance is safe and feasible
  • Potential for use in every patient before bariatric surgery (done during routine endoscopy)
  • Next step: test if using mucosal impedance for pre-surgical counseling and surgical choice impacts postoperative GERD outcomes
13. Summary: Key Themes Across All Presentations
  • AI augments but does not replace clinical expertise in esophageal disease management
  • Endoscopic endpoints may be more appropriate than histological endpoints for AI validation in low-prevalence diseases
  • Multi-modal data integration (clinical + endoscopic text + imaging) improves model performance
  • External validation and prospective trials remain critical next steps across all applications
